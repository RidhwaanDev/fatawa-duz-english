### TITLE
Saying "Our Islamic marriage is over"

### QUESTION
If a man said to his wife several times, "Our Islamic marriage is over", what is the ruling?

### ANSWER
In the case described, the jurists have counted these words — "Our Islamic marriage is over" — among the allusive expressions. So if he said them intending divorce, one divorce has taken effect upon his wife; and according to the explicit statement of the author of al-Baḥr, the divorce that takes effect by such words is a revocable one.

Consider what al-Baḥr al-Rāʾiq states:

وأشار بقوله تطلق إلى أن الواقع بهذه الكناية رجعي. (البحر الرائق: ۳/۳۰٦، كوئته).

So if he said them several times, and meant each utterance to establish a fresh divorce rather than merely to stress the first, three divorces will have taken effect.

Fatāwā Qāḍīkhān states:

لو قال لها: لا نكاح بيني وبينك أوقال: لم يبق بيني وبينك نكاح أوقال: فسخت نكاحك يقع الطلاق إذا نوى. (فتاوى قاضيخان على هامش الهندية: ۱/٤٦۸، فصل في الكنايات. وكذا في الفتاوى الهندية: ۱/۳۷۵، الفصل الخامس في الكنايات).

Our senior scholars, too, have held words of this kind to depend on the intention: if the intention is there the divorce takes effect, otherwise not.

In Fatāwā Dār al-ʿUlūm Deoband (complete, with evidences), the words "I have set you apart from being my wife" are accepted as bringing about a divorce provided the intention is there. See: (فتاوی دار العلوم دیوبند۹/۳۸۷، ۹/٤۷۱) ۔

See also that the words "I have cut off the spring of this woman's marriage" have been counted among the allusive expressions, (فتاوی دار العلوم دیوبند: ۹/٤۷۱) ۔ Of the words "You have gone out of being my wife" it has been ruled that a divorce takes effect provided the intention is there. (فتاوی دار العلوم دیوبند: ۹/۳۸۲) ۔ In Aḥsan al-Fatāwā, Muftī Rashīd Aḥmad Ludhiyānwī (may Allah have mercy on him) has likewise counted words like these among the allusive expressions, and has ruled that, given the intention, a revocable divorce takes effect. See: (احسن الفتاوی ۵/۱٤۲) ۔

A difficulty comes to mind here. Words about setting a woman apart from the marriage, or ending her being a wife, are almost explicit and are like the pronouncing of a divorce. How, then, can it be right to make the divorce depend on the intention with such words? They plainly deny the marriage as it stands, or else amount to a divorce. Nor is it easy to take these words as mere denial — that is, denying something one knows perfectly well to be true — since they are meant to end the marriage there and then.

The answer that comes to mind is that in common usage these words are used, while the marriage itself remains in place, for the absence of marital relations, or for the wife's keeping up relations with someone else. For example, when a woman has no relations with her husband, people say, "Our marriage is not with such-and-such a woman; she is really married to so-and-so." In short, the expression is used both for a marriage coming to an end and for marital relations and intimacy coming to an end. That is why the jurists have counted it among the allusive expressions.

Moreover, in "لا نکاح بیننا أو لست لي بزوجۃ" one possible sense is "لا نکاح بیننا لأن لکِ صلۃ وعلاقۃ بالغیر". If that is what is meant, no divorce takes place. But if what is meant is "لا نکاح بیننالأني طلقتکِ", then a revocable divorce takes effect.

واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی اعلم
