### TITLE
A woman in her waiting period leaving the house when a parent dies

### QUESTION
A woman is observing the waiting period after her husband's death, or after divorce. During the waiting period her mother or father dies. If she does not go to see her father or mother, she will carry the grief and regret for the rest of her life and will have to put up with reproaches. May she go for a short while to see her mother, or not? Please note that bringing the body to her is extremely difficult and burdensome.

### ANSWER
In the case described, one of her parents has died while she is in her waiting period. If she does not go, grief, regret and distress will weigh on her for the rest of her life, and there is a strong likelihood of taunts and criticism from the family. In both of these situations there is room for her to go out, because of the need and necessity involved. The jurists have permitted a widow to go out when there is a need.

There is also a narration in Sunan Ibn Mājah that the maternal aunt of Jābir (may Allah be pleased with him) was divorced. She wanted to go out to harvest her date palms, but a man stopped her. She came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and he said, "Go and harvest them; perhaps you will give something in charity or do some good."

This hadith too shows that a woman may go out for a genuine need, even though this was not a need for which no substitute could be found. Because the woman would have suffered distress, permission was given. But this permission does not mean that a woman in her waiting period may wander about in the name of visiting the sick and looking after them, and so ruin her waiting period.

Consider the following from Ibn Mājah:

عن جابر بن عبد الله رضي الله عنه قال: طلقت خالتي فأرادت أن تجد نخلها، فزجرها رجل أن تخرج إليه، فأتت النبي ﷺ فقال: بلي، فجدي فإنك عسى أن تصدقي أو تفعلي معروفاً. (ابن ماجة: ص ۴۶، باب هل تخرج المرأة في عدتها).

As regards going out at a time of need, the ruling is the same for a widow and for a divorced woman.

وأما الخروج للضرورة فلا فرق فيه بينهما كما نصوا عليه فيما يأتي

ولا يخرجان منه إلا أن تخرج أو يتهدم المنزل

ونحو ذلك من الضرورات الخ. (الدر المختار مع الشامي: ۳/۵۳۶، سعيد).

Al-Fatāwā al-ʿĀlamgīriyyah states:

إن اضطرت إلى الخروج من بيتها

فلا بأس عند ذلك أن تنتقل. (الفتاوى الهندية: ۱/۵۳۵).

Fatāwā Qāḍīkhān states:

الحرة المسلمة في عدة طلاق أو فرقة سوي الموت لا تخرج ليلاً ولا نهاراً إلا لضرورة

والمتوفي عنها زوجها تخرج بالنهار لحاجتها إلى النفقة ولا يبيت إلا في بيت زوجها

(فتاوى قاضيخان على هامش الهندية: ۱/۵۵۳، فصل فيما يحرم على المعتدة).

The passages quoted above make it quite clear that there is room for her to go out where there is a need. But one should also know what the limits of "need" are. Jadīd Fiqhī Mabāḥith says the following.

The root of this word is ضر. It is transmitted both with a fatḥah and with a ḍammah on the ḍād. Some lexicographers make no distinction between the two; others hold that with the ḍammah it is a noun and with the fatḥah a verbal noun. The word means harm, and is the opposite of benefit; this sense runs through every word derived from this root. (Khālid Sayfullāh Raḥmānī.) The word carries the sense of severe distress, severe loss and severe hardship.

Imam al-Shāṭibī (may Allah have mercy on him) sets out the technical definition in al-Muwāfaqāt:

فأما الضرورة فمعناها أنه لا بد منها في قيام مصالح الدين والدنيا بحيث إذا فقدت لم تجر مصالح الدنيا على استقامته، بل على فساد وتهارج وفوت حياة، وفي الآخرة فوت النجاة والنعيم والرجوع بالخسران المبين. (الموافقات: ۲/۴).

Some scholars are somewhat broader in how they define necessity: everything that would throw the order of life out of joint and give rise to corruption falls, for them, within the scope of necessity. Al-Shāṭibī's definition above reflects that tendency, and the definitions of Shaykh Abū Zahrah, Shaykh ʿAbd al-Wahhāb Khallāf and others also follow Abū Isḥāq al-Shāṭibī.

In the view of Mawlānā Khālid Sayfullāh, necessity is not merely a state of dire compulsion. Rather, the essentials of the various spheres of life, together with the permanent and temporary rulings laid down to safeguard them, all come under necessity. (جدید فقہی مباحث: ۱۴/۷، تلخیص مقالات ضرورت و حاجت) ۔

Qāmūs al-Fiqh states:

The fundamental aims of the sharia are five: preservation of religion, preservation of life (which includes a person's life, honour, standing in society and self-respect), preservation of lineage, preservation of property, and preservation of the intellect. Whatever these five aims depend on for their attainment and continuance is a necessity. Likewise, necessity is not confined to saving life; the essentials of every sphere of life come under it. (قاموس الفقہ: ۴/۳۱۳) ۔

The passages quoted above show that necessity is not confined to saving life. So if a woman in her waiting period, whether after divorce or after her husband's death, goes out to see the face of a parent who has died, there should be room for it. If she does not go, there is a real risk that the rest of her life will be blighted.

واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی اعلم
