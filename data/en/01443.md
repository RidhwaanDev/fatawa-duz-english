### TITLE
The claim that facing the Kaʿbah in prayer is worship of it

### QUESTION
Some non-Muslims object that, just as we face idols and worship them, Muslims too face the Kaʿbah and worship the Kaʿbah. What is the answer to this?

### ANSWER
We do not regard the Kaʿbah as an object of prostration or an object of worship. We only face the qiblah, and even that requirement lapses in certain situations. The reasons are as follows:

(1) When the direction of the qiblah is not known, one acts on his best judgement; and if after the prayer that judgement turns out to have been wrong, the prayer is still valid. Furthermore, in voluntary prayers outside a town facing the qiblah is not required: while travelling one may pray in whatever direction his mount is heading.

(2) The direction of the Kaʿbah has been fixed so as to preserve the unity of the Muslims throughout the world.

(3) If the Kaʿbah itself were the thing intended, it would have been permitted to pray facing a picture of it — yet that is not so.

(4) It is valid to pray inside the Kaʿbah and on its roof, even though one's back is then turned towards part of the Kaʿbah.

(5) When praying in an aeroplane only the direction is taken into account; otherwise the Kaʿbah is far below the aircraft.

(6) If the Kaʿbah were — Allah forbid — demolished, prayer towards its direction would still be valid.

(7) For a person living outside Makkah al-Mukarramah, the qiblah is not the Kaʿbah itself but the direction of the Kaʿbah.

In Maʿārif al-Qurʾān, Ḥaḍrat Mawlānā Idrīs Ṣāḥib writes:

We do not worship the Kaʿbah or offer devotion to it, nor do we regard the Kaʿbah as an object of worship or of prostration. We worship God alone; we simply turn our faces towards the qiblah. The very words used bear witness to this: "facing the Kaʿbah" and "idol-worship". "Facing" means nothing more than turning towards something, whereas "idol-worship" means that the idols are objects of worship. Had Muslims held that the Kaʿbah is to be worshipped, the objection would have been justified.

Second: in the view of Muslims, for the prayer to be valid it is enough that one's face be turned towards the Kaʿbah; even an intention to face it is not required. What is intended is the One worshipped, and an intention directed at Him is required. The direction of the qiblah is only a means to that, and intending a means is not required. Had the Kaʿbah — Allah forbid — been the object of worship, an intention directed at the object of worship would have been necessary.

Third: from the beginning of the prayer to its end there is not a single word that indicates veneration of the Kaʿbah. From beginning to end, every word and every action expresses the glorification and greatness of Allah Most High, His praise, and His sanctification. From first to last the Kaʿbah is not so much as named. In idol-worship, by contrast, the veneration is directed at those stones and figures which you call Mahādev, Shiv and the like. In short, what comparison is there between idol-worship and the prayer?

Fourth: the Kaʿbah is not the name of those bricks and stones, nor does the prayer stop when the Kaʿbah is demolished. That is why, during the period when the Kaʿbah and its walls were pulled down, the prayer continued exactly as before. Had the wall of the Kaʿbah been the object of prostration, of worship, or of intent, prayer would have been suspended in that period; at the very most, the worship of the days that had passed would have been made up after the rebuilding. In idol-worship it is plain that the idols themselves are the objects of worship, of prostration, and of intent. This is why, if the idols are taken out of a Shiva temple or any other temple and set down somewhere else, all the rites are thereafter performed in the new place and nobody gives the first building a thought. It is also why a man's prayer is valid if he prays on the roof of the Kaʿbah. Had the Kaʿbah been the object of Muslim worship, prayer on top of it would not have been valid, since the Kaʿbah is then no longer in front of him, and because climbing on top of an object of worship is disrespectful. The objectors have presumably judged us by themselves, for they regard the cow and the ox as deities and objects of worship, and then ride them, load goods on them, and beat them from behind with sticks when the deity will not move quickly enough; "بریں عقل دانش بباید گریست" — at such wisdom one can only weep.

Fifth: Muslims call the Kaʿbah the House of Allah; they do not call it Allah or God. And it is obvious that when a person goes to a house, it is the occupant who is intended, and everyone understands the courtesies and homage offered there to be meant for the master of the house. It is like bowing towards a throne in salutation: the salutation is for the one who owns the throne, not for the throne itself. Idol-worshippers, on the other hand, do not regard their idols as God's house or God's throne; rather the idols themselves are the object of intent and of worship, and they worship them as Mahādev, Shiv, Ganesh and the like.
