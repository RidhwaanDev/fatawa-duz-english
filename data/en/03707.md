### TITLE
The ruling on a term in a loan being binding by way of a promise

### QUESTION
Zayd needed a large sum of money for a court case. Because of his relationship with Bakr, Bakr lent Zayd three hundred thousand rand on the promise of repayment in instalments over a period of two years. Then relations suddenly came to an end, and Bakr demanded the return of the money within six months. Zayd, reminding him of the promise, said that the repayment would be completed within the two-year period. Since Bakr is a scholar, he said that a term is not binding in a loan and that he has the right to demand repayment. What is the ruling of the Sharīʿah now?

2. Zayd also says: I have no arrangement for repayment at present, so granting a respite is binding upon you. What is the ruling in the Sharīʿah? Will granting a respite be binding or not? Explain with the evidence and be rewarded with an abundant reward.

### ANSWER
According to the school of the Ḥanafīs a term is not binding in a loan, so Bakr has the right to demand repayment; but there will be the sin of breaking a promise without cause.

2. If Zayd has no arrangement for repayment at present and is in straitened circumstances, then granting him a respite is binding upon Bakr. Consider the following. It is mentioned in al-Hidāyah:

قال: وكل دين حال إذا أجله صاحبه صار مؤجلاً لما ذكرنا إلا القرض فإن تأجيله لا يصح لأنه إعارة وصلة في الابتداء حتى يصح بلفظة الإعارة ولا يملكه من لا يملك التبرع كالوصي والصبي ومعاوضة في الانتهاء فعلي اعتبار الابتداء لا يلزم التأجيل فيه. (الهداية: ۳/٦۰).
(وكذا في التبيين: ٤/۸٤، ط: إمداديه، وفتاوى الشامي: ۵/۱۵۸، سعيد، ومجمع الأنهر: ۳/۱۱۷، ط: بيروت، والبحر الرائق: ٦/۱۳۲، ط: دار المعرفة).

It is recorded in Bihishtī Zēwar:

Issue no. 4: If a person borrowed some money or grain from someone on the promise that "we shall repay it after a month or after fifteen days," and the lender accepted this, even then the stipulation of a term is void — indeed, it is impermissible. If he (the lender) has need before that term and asks you for it, or even asks for it without need, you will have to give it at once. (Bihishtī Zēwar, part 5/407).

Aḥsan al-Fatāwā states:

A creditor may demand repayment before the appointed term; however, if he demands it without need, there will be the sin of breaking a promise. (Aḥsan al-Fatāwā: 7/177, published by Saʿīd).

But Mawlānā Khālid Sayf Allāh Ṣāḥib has written that the term in a loan ought to be taken into account.

Consider the following. Qāmūs al-Fiqh states:

The third issue relating to the repayment of a loan is this: when does repayment of the loan become obligatory? According to the Ḥanafīs, the Shāfiʿīs and the Ḥanbalīs, the lender is not bound by whatever term is fixed in the loan, because a loan is a kind of gratuitous favour, and if the term were binding it would no longer remain a gratuitous favour. According to the Mālikīs, both parties will be bound by that term, and the lender will not have the right to demand the loan before the fixed term; ʿAllāmah Ibn al-Qayyim (may Allah have mercy on him) inclines to this view. The fact is that in this issue the position of the Mālikīs appears closer to what is correct, and this is what is required by the statements transmitted in the Qurʾān and the hadith concerning the fulfilment of conditions and promises. It should also be borne in mind that even those jurists who did not hold the respite of a fixed term in a loan to be binding, held this ruling in terms of judicial enforcement; as a matter of religious conscience, the fulfilment of a covenant is obligatory in the view of all the jurists. ʿAllāmah Ibn Taymiyyah (may Allah have mercy on him) also inclines in this matter to the same view as the Mālikīs. (Qāmūs al-Fiqh: 4/492, under the heading: "When does the repayment of a loan become obligatory", published by Zamzam).

It is recorded in ʿAṭr-e-Hidāyah:

He has forbidden making a term binding in a loan. In the marginal note it is recorded:

Fourthly, bindingness too cannot be ruled out, because the benefit belongs to the debtor; and it is related from Imām Mālik (may Allah have mercy on him) that if the judge fixes a term, it becomes binding. A loan, however, does not by itself call for the bindingness of a condition, since it is a gratuitous favour; yet there is no objection to bindingness arising from a promise and the like. So this bindingness is not by virtue of the loan alone but by virtue of the promise, and the jurists have indeed accepted a promise as binding on account of need. (Ḥāshiyah ʿAṭr-e-Hidāyah, p. 294, published by Zamzam).
