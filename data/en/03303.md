### TITLE
The purity or impurity of snake venom and the ruling on using it

### QUESTION
I would like an investigation into snake venom: is it pure or impure? And is it permissible to use it or not? Will there be any difference between external and internal use? Snake venom is used in some medicines and in some cosmetics; is it permissible to use such things or not? بینوا بالتفصیل

### ANSWER
The skin, flesh and venom of a snake are all impure, and their external and internal use is impermissible, because venom combines three things: impurity, foulness and harm. If, however, its reality and essence are altered by being put into medicines, and these three things — impurity, foulness and harm — are removed, then the external and internal use of such medicines is permissible and correct. Consider what Badāʾiʿ al-Ṣanāʾiʿ records:

وأما الذي يعيش في البر فأنواع ثلاثة: ما ليس له دم أصلاً، وما ليس له دم سائل، وما له دم سائل، مثل الجراد والزنبور والذباب والعنكبوت

لا يحل أكله إلا الجراد خاصة؛ لأنها من الخبائث لاستبعاد الطباع السليمة إياها وقد قال الله تبارك وتعالى: ﴿ويحرم عليهم الخبائث﴾

وكذلك ما ليس له دم سائل مثل الحية والوزغ

وجميع الحشرات وهوام الأرض، من الفار والقراد والقنافذ

ولا خلاف في حرمة هذه الأشياء إلا في الضب فإنه حلال عند الشافعي رحمه الله. (بدائع الصنائع: ۵/۳٦، سعيد).

Al-Fatāwā al-Hindiyyah states:

جلد الحية نجس وإن كانت مذبوحة؛ لأنه لا يحتمل الدباغة، هكذا في الظهيرية. (الفتاوى الهندية: ۱/٤٦).
(In our own day, however, tanning by means of chemicals is possible, and so it will become pure.)

ʿAllāmah Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī al-Shāfiʿī (may Allah have mercy on him) writes in his Fatāwā:

وسئل: أمدنا الله من مدده

هل سم الحيات ونحوها نجس؟
فأجاب: أفاض الله علي من فيض مدده

سم الحيات نجس كما صرح به جمع متقدمون ومتأخرون،
واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی أعلم
. (الفتاوى الفقهية الكبرى: ۱/۲۸، دار الفكر).

Asnā al-Maṭālib states:

وممن صرح بنجاسة سم الحيات العجلي. (أسني المطالب: ۱/۱۷۲، دار الكتاب الإسلامي).

One of the grounds of prohibition is poison itself: since it is harmful to the human body, it is forbidden on that account.

Consider what al-Intifāʿ bi'l-Aʿyān al-Muḥarramah states:

الأسباب الشرعية العامة في التحريم؛ تختلف الأسباب الكامنة وراء تحريم أي عين من الأعيان وتتعدد بحسب ماهية العين المحرمة، ويمكن إجمال تلك الأسباب بنقاط كما يأتي: أولاً: الأعيان المحرمة بسبب الضرر، كثيرة ولا حصر لها، من ذلك الأعيان السامة سواء أكانت السموم فيها مضرة على الفور كبعض أنواع من العقارب والحيات أم كانت سمومها ملحقة للإنسان بالضرر على المدي الطويل كالدخان. (الانتفاع بالأعيان المحرمة، ص ۲٦، ط: دار النفائس اردن).

Bihishtī Zewar states: One should know that in the pure sharia there are four grounds on which the use of a thing is forbidden: 1. impurity, as with urine and excrement; 2. being harmful, as with arsenic; 3. foulness — that is, that a sound nature is revolted by it, as with insects and creeping things; 4. causing intoxication. (Bihishtī Zewar, p. 98, part nine, Dār al-Ishāʿat, Karachi)

Imdād al-Fatāwā states:

In our part of the country the gall bladder of a python or a snake is very commonly given to children as medicine; indeed both the mother and the child are made to take it. Can this be permissible in any way or not? There is scarcely anyone here who has not taken it.

Answer: It is not permissible. On this the annotator, Muftī Shabbīr Aḥmad Qāsimī, writes:

This is because treating oneself with a filthy and impure substance is impermissible according to the established position of the school. The permission the later jurists gave for treatment with something forbidden was given on the condition that a doctor states that the impure medicine is beneficial and that no other lawful and pure medicine can take its place; in that case the impure medicine may be used. From the case put in the question, however, it appears that whether there is a need or not, the gall bladder is fed to the children and the mother in every case. And since this gall bladder is a filthy and impure substance — for the gall bladder of every living creature has the same ruling as its urine — it is not permissible to use it without necessity. (Imdād al-Fatāwā with the annotations: 9/264, Zakariyyā Book Depot, India)

If, however, putting it into medicines brings about a transformation of the substance itself, then its use will be permitted, since such a transformation is one of the things that render a substance pure. Consider what the accomplished scholar Ibn al-Humām (may Allah have mercy on him) says in Fatḥ al-Qadīr:

وكثير من المشايخ اختاروا قول محمد رحمه الله وهو المختار لأن الشرع رتب وصف النجاسة على تلك الحقيقة، وتنتفي الحقيقة بانتفاء بعض أجزاء مفهومها فكيف بالكل؟ فإن الملح غير العظم واللحم، فإذا صار ملحاً ترتب حكم الملح. ونظيره في الشرع: النطفة نجسة وتصير علقة وهي نجسة، وتصير مضغة فتطهر، والعصير طاهر فيصير خمراً فينجس، ويصير خلاً فيطهر، فعرفنا أن استحالة العين تستتبع زوال الوصف المرتب عليها. وعلى قول محمد رحمه الله فرعوا الحكم بطهارة صابون صنع من زيت نجس. (فتح القدير: ۱/۲۰۰، دار الفكر).
قال ابن رشد: إنه قد علم من ضرورة الشرع أن الأحكام المختلفة إنما هي لذوات المختلفة، وأن الخمر غير ذات الخل، والخل بإجماع حلال، فإذا انتقلت ذات خمر إلى ذات الخل، وجب أن يكون حلالاً كيفما انتقل. (بداية المجتهد: ۱/۳٤۹، الأطعمة).

Muftī Taqī says:

الخمائر والجلاتين المتخذة من الخنزير: إن كان العنصر المستخلص من الخنزير تستحيل ماهيته بعملة كيمياوية، بحيث تنقلب حقيقته تماماً، زالت حرمته ونجاسته، وإن لم تنقلب حقيقته بقي على حرمته ونجاسته، لأن انقلاب الحقيقة مؤثر في زوال الطهارة والحرمة عند الحنفية. (بحوث في قضايا فقهية معاصرة: ۱/۳٤۱، مكتبة دار العلوم كراتشي).

On the transformation of a reality, the writing of Ḥaḍrat Muftī Kifāyatullāh (may Allah have mercy on him) is illuminating. Consider it:

Transformation of reality means that a thing itself abandons its own reality and is changed into some other reality — as when wine becomes vinegar, or blood becomes musk, or a drop of seminal fluid becomes a clot of flesh, and so forth. In these cases the wine has itself abandoned its reality as wine, the blood its reality as blood, and the seminal fluid its reality as seminal fluid, and each has been changed into another reality. The ruling that the reality has changed can only be given when the properties peculiar to the first, now transformed, reality no longer remain in it — as is found in the examples mentioned, for once wine has become vinegar the properties peculiar to wine disappear entirely.

The disappearance of some of the properties, or their not being perceptible because so little of them is left, does not amount to a transformation. The jurists have stated this expressly: if some wine is mixed into flour, the dough kneaded and bread baked from it, that bread is impure; and if a tola or two of wine or urine is mixed into a pitcher or two of water, that water is impure — even though no trace of so small a quantity of wine would be perceptible in the bread or the water. But since in these cases the wine has not itself given up its own reality, the ruling of impurity remains, and its not being perceptible is only because its parts were few. Since the parts of wine were few and those of the flour many, it is not perceptible in the bread. This, then, is a mixture, not a transformation.

In the same way, the survival of certain qualities of the transformed reality that are not peculiar to it does not prevent transformation. For instance, even after wine has become vinegar its thinness remains, and some greasiness of the impure oil remains in soap; for thinness is not peculiar to the reality of wine, nor greasiness to the reality of oil. Great thought and caution are therefore necessary when ruling that the rulings have changed because of a transformation of the substance, for transformation and mixture are often confused, and a transformation is taken to be a mixture, or a mixture a transformation. واللہ الموفق (Kifāyat al-Muftī: 2/333, Dār al-Ishāʿat, Karachi)

Ḥakīm al-Ummah Ḥaḍrat Mawlānā Ashraf ʿAlī Thānwī (may Allah illuminate his resting place) writes:

An account of the change of essence: with a change of essence the rulings change as well. For example, grape juice is pure; but when it becomes another thing, namely wine, it becomes impure; and when the wine in turn becomes another thing, namely vinegar, it becomes pure. What a change of essence means is that one thing turns into another thing whose ruling is entirely the opposite of the first. Thus an impure thing is transformed into something pure, and so the impure thing becomes pure: manure is impure, but once it has turned into earth, and earth is a pure thing, it has become pure. Or an egg is pure, but it turns into blood, and blood is an impure thing, so the egg has become impure; and when that blood turns into a lump of flesh, and flesh is pure, it becomes pure again. But if the transformation is into something whose ruling is the same as its ruling before the transformation, then that same ruling remains: if it was pure it stays pure, and if it was impure it stays impure. For example, a pure bone is burnt and turns to ash: a transformation has taken place, but the ruling is unchanged, because ash too is pure. And if a drop of seminal fluid turns into blood, a transformation has taken place, but from something impure into something impure, and the ruling remains as it was; whereas when the clot becomes flesh it becomes pure, since a lump of flesh is pure. And if the transformation is incomplete — that is, the second thing has not become something different from the first, and only a change of a sort has occurred — then the rulings will not change. For example, bread is baked from impure wheat, so that in place of the form of wheat the form of bread has come about; but this is not reckoned to be its having become another thing. (Bihishtī Zewar, part nine, p. 782, Dār al-Ishāʿat)
