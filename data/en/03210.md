### TITLE
The ruling on Islamic takāful or mutual assistance, and the difference between insurance and takāful

### QUESTION
What is the reality of non-Islamic insurance? In place of it, the honourable muftis and great scholars have devised a system under the name of Islamic takāful or Islamic mutual assistance (muwāsāt). What in summary is the reality of that system, and what is the difference between the two? بینوا توجروا

### ANSWER
The reality of non-Islamic insurance, whether it takes the form of life insurance or of general insurance, is one and the same, and it is impermissible. In brief, its reality is this: a person exposed to the risk of accidents — technically called the policy-holder — goes to an insurance company and says: I work on a machine in such-and-such a factory and I am constantly exposed to the risk of an accident, that while working at the machine I may lose my life or that some limb may be severed so that I am left unfit for work, and that there will then be no one to look after my wife and children and meet their expenses. I therefore wish to insure my life or my limbs, so that if some sudden calamity should occur I may at least have some support left to me. That is an example of life insurance.

An example of insurance of property is this: a man drives a taxi, say, and has no other source of income. He is anxious that if the taxi should ever be destroyed in an accident his life will be ruined and he will be left without support. So he comes to an insurance company and says: if I should meet with an accident, let me be given such assistance as will enable me and my wife and children to live; and whatever payment I have to make you for this, I will pay it now.

The company then, on its own particular calculations, tells him that for such-and-such a policy he will have to pay five thousand rand a month, and that in the event of an accident it will pay him one hundred thousand rand under one policy and fifty thousand rand under another. It should be borne in mind that accidents do not befall all of those who take part in the company and pay it money. Even after making good certain losses, therefore, the money of a great many people is left with the company overall, and the company counts this as its profit. This is the secret of the companies' profits.

The defects of the customary insurance companies from the point of view of the Sacred Law:

1. Interest is present in it. This is because, if a person meets with an accident, he receives a sum far greater than the amount he has paid in, and this is interest — for interest is precisely this, that in an exchange one of the contracting parties pays out more than what he received. Hidāyah records: "

الربا هو الفضل المستحق لأحد المتعاقدين في المعاوضة الخالي عن عوض شرط فيه". (الهداية: ۳/۷۸).

2. Gambling is present in it, in this way: both parties stand at risk of losing their money. If no accident occurs, the money of the insured is lost; and if an accident does occur, the company suffers the loss, because what it has received from that man is far less than the sum it will have to pay out. This is exactly what is called gambling;

لأن القمار هو تعليق ملك على الخطر والمال من جانبين. (معجم لغات الفقهاء، ص ۳٦۹).

3. Another defect is that a person who has taken out insurance, tempted by the gain, himself destroys the property he has insured in order to obtain the money.

In place of this, the honourable scholars have devised a system under the name of Islamic takāful, in which there is deliverance from such curses as interest and gambling.

The need for Islamic takāful:

In this fast-moving life a person is constantly meeting with difficulties. As a rule he manages somehow to free himself from small obstacles, but at times he is left helpless in the face of great and sudden disasters; and sometimes a man is so destitute that, watching the labour of years go to waste, he begins to despair of life itself. In such circumstances, to have compassion on the afflicted is a demand of human nature and of reason and the Sacred Law alike. In the Islamic Sacred Law it is difficult even to count the exhortations to it and the statements of its merits. It is for this reason that we see people — sometimes generally, and sometimes the people of a particular district or a particular trade — joining together and setting up a relief fund in order to assist those struck by accidents and to make good their losses. But this help and assistance generally comes only after the accident has occurred; and since there is no right to demand it, the afflicted person has to accept it with embarrassment. Nor is it regulated or certain, so that sometimes the afflicted person has to ask for it, and sometimes even asking brings him nothing. In any case, in order to spare the afflicted person all this, a better form of mutual assistance can be devised: those exposed to danger may join together and set up a relief fund beforehand out of their respective contributions; then, whenever one of the members who have built up the fund meets with an accident, he is helped from the fund according to the rules. In this form, since the fund will already be in existence, the afflicted person will have a kind of assurance and certainty. And since this fund has been built up out of their own private contributions and is not the property of any particular member, claiming help from the fund after an accident and receiving it will neither resemble begging nor be a matter of embarrassment. Such a fund will be, as it were, a small public treasury which is nobody's property, and in which, according to the rules, various classes of people will have the right to claim assistance.

A precedent in the Sacred Law for setting up a relief fund:

The very idea of setting up relief funds of this kind before danger arises, and thereby gaining peace of mind, existed in the time of the Prophet ﷺ himself. There is a hadith in the two Ṣaḥīḥs:

عن أبي بردة عن أبي موسى قال: قال النبي ﷺ: "إن الأشعريين إذا أرملوا في الغزو، أو قل طعام عيالهم بالمدينة جمعوا ما كان عندهم في ثوب واحد، ثم اقتسموه بينهم في إناء واحد بالسوية فهم مني وأنا منهم". (صحيح البخاري، رقم: ۲٤۸٦، باب الشركة في الطعام).

That is: when the provisions of the Ashʿarīs run out on a campaign, or when food is found to be short while they are living in their own city, they gather whatever food or provisions each of them has into a single cloth, and then divide it equally among themselves by means of a single vessel. So these people are of me and I am of them.

Imām al-Bukhārī (may Allah have mercy on him) has likewise quoted a hadith of Ḥaḍrat Jābir (may Allah be pleased with him):

عن جابر بن عبد الله رضي الله عنه أنه قال: بعث رسول الله ﷺ بعثاً قبل الساحل فأمر عليهم أبا عبيدة بن الجراح وهم ثلاث مائة وأنا فيهم فخرجنا حتى إذا كنا ببعض الطريق فني الزاد فأمر أبو عبيدة بأزواد ذلك الجيش فجمع ذلك كله فكان مزودي تمر فكان يقوتنا كل يوم قليلاً قليلاً حتى فني فلم يكن يصيبنا إلا تمرة تمرة. (صحيح البخاري، رقم: ۲٤۸۳، باب الشركة في الطعام).

Imām al-Bukhārī (may Allah have mercy on him) has recorded several incidents of this kind in his Ṣaḥīḥ. Reflection on all of them shows that they acted in this way at a time when there was a shortage of provisions among the fighters or in the tribe, and not everyone had the supplies he needed: some had them and some did not. It was then feared that those who had less than they needed would fall into hardship once their provisions ran out, and that those who had more would not even come to know of it — for it may well be that companions in difficulty would not disclose their condition to others so as to avoid the humiliation of asking. And even if the others did somehow come to know of it, the danger remained that no orderly solution could be arranged at that point, since it is possible that the companions who had more provisions would by then have spent freely and used up a good deal of what they had. Had the arrangement been made beforehand, those who had provisions would have spent only what was sufficient and would have been able to help those with nothing out of the remainder. In the incidents mentioned, therefore, great foresight was shown: before the danger arose, everyone's provisions were gathered together and then divided equally among all. As a result the danger to those with no provisions or with few was removed and all shared alike in ease and in hardship, and the blameworthy situation did not arise in which some passed their time in distress while others lived in comfort and peace. It should be borne in mind that this incident is not identical in every respect with the relief fund proposed here; it is presented only by way of support.

Another example of the same thing is found in the story of Ḥaḍrat Yūsuf (peace be upon him): before the famine he gathered in the produce of the country and distributed it as it was needed, and by this means the people were saved from the danger of destruction. Had this not been done, many people would have perished in that famine while certain others passed their lives in luxury and ease — which is blameworthy in reason and in the Sacred Law alike.

In any case, keeping the above principle of the noble Sacred Law in view: if people exposed to the risk of accidents join together and, before any accident occurs, build up a relief fund out of joint contributions, and then, after an accident occurs, provide assistance to the afflicted member according to the rules, then — if Allah Most High wills — this undertaking and arrangement will not be impermissible in the eyes of the pure Sacred Law; on the contrary, it will be counted a commendable undertaking and system. And when, by means of it, people are also saved from the curse of the interest and gambling found in Western insurance, its merit becomes greater still, and no one will be left with anything to say against its permissibility and its commendability.

Some step-by-step measures for Islamic mutual assistance:

Suppose that a thousand bus operators come together and jointly set up a fund in view of the risks they face. First of all they estimate how many vehicles ordinarily meet with accidents in a year, and the estimate arrived at is that about two vehicles meet with accidents in a year. If each of the two vehicles is worth two million rupees, the two together are worth four million. Before setting up the organisation, therefore, they will have to collect four million; and besides this, a further sum will have to be collected for the remaining expenses of the office, the staff and so on. Moreover, the calculation made for accidents may prove too low or too high, so it is decided to collect, say, five million. Now, five million divided among a thousand men comes to five thousand for each. This is collected and the fund is formed. Ordinarily no one will find any difficulty in paying so small a contribution, especially since the benefit of it comes to them. Then, whenever one of the participants meets with an accident, his loss will be made good out of this donation fund. And since everyone is certainly exposed to the danger of accidents, why should he not be willing to give this small amount?

But in order to achieve the aims of this organisation, several further matters will have to be seen to: the safeguarding of the donation fund; maintaining contact on the organisation's behalf with whichever of its members suffers a loss; the setting up of an office to carry out these tasks; and the staff needed to run the office. Now it is obvious that the amount of the donation fund is limited and that, after bearing all these expenses, the fund will be empty in a short while. The prudent course, therefore, will be to invest it in some lawful trade, from which the employees will continue to receive their salaries, while whatever profit remains is added to the principal fund and keeps strengthening it.

The question now arises: how is it to be invested in trade? One way would be that the task is entrusted to one of the office staff, so that on the one hand he supervises the work of the takāful and on the other he carries on the trade.

A second way is that the money of this fund be handed over to some honest and capable person by way of muḍārabah or mushārakah, on the basis that a share of the profit goes to him and the rest to the fund, and that if there is a loss the fund will bear it. Accordingly, a man should be sought who is not only honest but also expert in trade and willing to supervise the office; he will carry on trade and earn a profit, put the remainder into the fund, and at the same time run the affairs of the fund. In this same arrangement, if the participants in the Islamic insurance scheme, in order to strengthen the business, also give this man some capital of their own besides the fund by way of muḍārabah, the profit of which they share between them, and if they all see a profit in it, then this too is permissible.

The superiority of Islamic mutual assistance over non-Islamic insurance, and the difference between the two:

In Western-style insurance companies, the premium or instalment money that the insured pays in becomes the property of the company (that is, of the individuals who set the company up), and the company counts it as its own profit. Then, when the man who has paid the instalments meets with an accident and the company has to make good his loss, it regards itself as making good that loss out of its own wealth and counts it as its own loss. Western insurance companies are therefore always trying to find some way of denying the claims of those who have met with accidents, so that they will not have to pay compensation; for the less compensation they have to pay, the greater the company's profit. That is why such companies keep adding to their conditions and stipulations day by day and keep making their procedures more and more complicated. In Islamic insurance, by contrast, the very purpose of the arrangement is to help those in distress, and this help will come from a relief fund that is not the company's property at all. The company will therefore never try to withhold it, since there is no advantage in it for them, and it will help those in distress open-heartedly.

The second great merit of Islamic mutual assistance is this: of the funds these companies hold — the relief fund and the trading fund — the company owns neither. The companies' profits will come solely and entirely from the trading carried on with the two funds. Islamic mutual assistance companies will therefore always try to invest this money in the most profitable trade possible, or to set up factories with it and earn a profit thereby, since they have no other course open to them. So when a large capital of the Islamic insurance scheme is set in motion in the form of trade or of a factory, the benefit of it will spread in every direction, and companies, policy-holders, the workers connected with that trade, and the fund itself will all benefit at once — unlike the Western-style insurance company, whose profit consists of the instalments people have paid in, of which it has become the owner and which it is constantly scheming to swallow up by depriving those in distress.

A few superficial doubts about the outline of Islamic mutual assistance presented above:

Some people say that, so far as the outcome is concerned, no difference is apparent between non-Islamic insurance and Islamic insurance: there more money is received and it is called interest, and here too more money is received but it is called profit. There too, if no accident occurs nothing is received, and if an accident occurs the afflicted person receives, along with his own money, the money of others as well; and exactly the same thing happens here, in that he takes the money of others.

At a superficial glance a resemblance to interest and gambling may indeed appear in Islamic insurance, but in reality there is neither interest nor gambling in it, because the basis and the reality of the cooperative fund described are quite distinct. In interest, money is given and more is taken in return for the delay, whereas in Islamic insurance no one gives money to anyone; rather he leaves his money as a contribution, so that his ownership of it comes to an end — the object being that the fund should be built up before the accident rather than after it. Moreover, in interest the giver's aim is to receive a profit, whereas here no one wants to meet with an accident and suffer hardship in order to receive money from the fund; on the contrary, everyone wants that no accident should occur at all. Let us give an example. In the view of the jurists, the sale of a dirham for a dirham on deferred terms is interest and unlawful; yet to lend dirhams and receive dirhams back on deferred terms is permissible in everyone's view — although, so far as the outcome is concerned, in both cases there is an exchange of dirhams on deferred terms. Some scholars hold that a loan is excepted from this sale of dirhams for dirhams on deferred terms on account of need; but the correct position is that although the two look alike, their realities are entirely different: in the sale of dirhams the aim is to obtain a profit, whereas in a loan the aim is to confer a benefit.

In the same way we say that in interest the aim is to obtain a profit, while in Islamic insurance it is to confer a benefit. This difference is established by the very hadiths we have mentioned above, in which the provisions of all the companions were collected and divided equally among them, so that some received less than the goods they had given and some received more; yet no one called this interest. Rather, it was counted as belonging to the chapter of good social conduct and kindly dealing. Thus see what ʿAllāmah al-Nawawī (may Allah have mercy on him) says under the hadith of Ḥaḍrat Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī (may Allah be pleased with him):

وفي هذا الحديث فضيلة الأشعريين وفضيلة الإيثار والمواساة وفضيلة خلط الأزواد في السفر وفضيلة جمعها في شيء عند قلتها في الحضر ثم يقسم وليس المراد بهذا القسمة المعروفة في كتب الفقه بشروطها ومنعها في الربويات واشتراط المواساة وغيرها وإنما المراد هنا إباحة بعضهم بعضاً ومواساتهم بالموجود. (شرح النووي على مسلم: باب من فضائل الأشعريين: ۲/۳۰۳، ط: فيصل). (وكذا في فتح الباري: ۵/۱۳۰).

In the same way, there is a very great difference between the reality of Islamic insurance and that of gambling. Gambling is defined as:

تعليق الملك على الخطر والمال في الجانبين. (قواعد الفقه، ص ٤۳٤، ط: دار الكتاب، ديوبند)،

and its object is to obtain the greatest possible profit. The gambler's whole effort is to win and to carry off the wealth of others along with his own; and when he loses, shame and regret overcome him and his heart is unwilling to hand over the money. It is precisely this that in the end becomes a cause of rancour and enmity between them, and this is the spirit and the essence of gambling, as is stated in the Noble Qurʾān:

﴿إنما يريد الشيطان أن يوقع بينكم العداوة والبغضاء في الخمر والميسر ويصدكم عن ذكر الله وعن الصلاة فهل أنتم منتهون﴾ [المائدة: ۹۱].

In Islamic insurance, on the other hand, none of these things is found. No one wishes to meet with an accident so as to carry off all the money; and if some other person receives that money, no one feels any regret that he did not receive it himself, because from the outset he gave the money as a donation and it had passed out of his ownership. Sometimes, indeed, a person is glad that someone else has received it, since he has had a share in helping people who are in distress and destitute. (Derived from Asās-e Muwāsāt-e Islāmiyyah, by Muftī Kifāyat Allāh of Bangladesh.)

The upshot is that insurance is a combination of interest and gambling, in which the insured and the owners of the company are each seeking their own advantage; whereas in Islamic takāful a contribution from the members is held in trust and serves the participants at the time of need. Or, to put it another way, in insurance a man lends money to the company, and so it becomes a case of

"كل قرض جر نفعاً"

whereas in takāful no one lends anything to anyone; rather a contribution is collected.

واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی اعلم
