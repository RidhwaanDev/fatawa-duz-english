### TITLE
Medical aid schemes

### QUESTION
Is medical aid permissible or not?

### ANSWER
Medical aid, which is current nowadays in non-Muslim countries, ought to be permissible as a contract of hire (ijārah), because the substance of this contract is that one party pays a specified, fixed sum while the other party takes responsibility for his medical treatment. Two parallels for it are set out below.

(1) In the towns and villages of India and Pakistan — indeed in the cities too — this arrangement has been current for centuries: the barber, the blacksmith, the carpenter and others take responsibility for the work people need of them, and the people give them a share of the crop, or some money, when the harvest comes in. This practice was current everywhere: in Afghanistan, in India and Pakistan, in Deoband, Thānah Bhawan and Saharanpur. The barber undertakes to keep the hair of the whole household in order, which is an undefined undertaking, yet it gives rise to no dispute, and people give him grain or some money when the harvest comes. In medical aid too, one party takes responsibility for treatment. In both there is indefiniteness in the work to be done, and it is not a cause of dispute.

Al-Hidāyah states that if a person appoints someone as his agent to conclude a settlement on his behalf — in a case of intentional homicide, for example — and the agent concludes the settlement, the settlement money is not binding upon the agent; but if the agent accepted responsibility for that sum, the sum becomes binding upon him. It then says:

"لأنه حينئذٍ مؤاخذ بعقد الضمان". (الهداية: ۳/۲۵۰، باب التبرع بالصلح والتوكيل به).

(2) Engaging a wet-nurse for children is permissible by agreement. Here too, one party undertakes the responsibility of suckling and so forth, while the other party pays a salary or hire. The times of feeding are all undefined, but this indefiniteness is not a cause of dispute, and so it is permissible by agreement.

An objection: One objection does arise, namely whether the party that undertakes responsibility for treatment is a private employee (ajīr khāṣṣ) or a common hireling (ajīr mushtarak). If he is a private employee, how does he take on responsibility for other people as well, seeing that a private employee does not work for others?

"والخاص لا يمكنه أن يعمل لغيره لأن منافعه في المدة صارت مستحقة للمستأجر والأجر مقابل بالمنافع". (فتاوى الشامي: ٦/٦٤، باب ضمان الأجير، سعيد).

And if he is a common hireling, he is entitled to payment only once he has done the work — whereas in medical aid he receives his payment even if he does no work in that month.

The answer: The answer is that some of the jurists have combined the private employee and the common hireling in certain cases. For instance, if a wet-nurse suckles the child in her own house she is both a private employee and a common hireling: that is, she receives the wage for suckling even though she suckles other children as well.

Fatḥ al-Muʿīn, the commentary on al-Kanz, states:

والحاصل أن المسائل في الظئر تعارضت فمنها ما يدل على أنها في معني أجير الوحد كقولهم لعدم الضمان في هذه، ومنها ما يدل على أنها في معني المشترك كقولهم إنها تستحق الأجر على الفريقين إذا أجرت نفسها لهما، قال الاتقاني: والصحيح أنه إن دفع الولد إليها لترضعه فهي أجير مشترك وإن حملها إلى منزله فهي أجير وحد وقال في العناية: وذكر في الذخيرة ما يدل على أنها يجوز أن تكون خاصاً ومشتركاً حتى لو أجرت نفسها لغيره استحقت الأجر على الفريقين كاملاً عملاً بشبه الأجير المشترك وتأثم نظراً إلى أن لها شبهاً بالأجير الخاص. (فتح المعين: ۳/۲۵٤، وكذا في فتاوى الشامي: ٦/۷۱، سعيد).

أقول: ويرتفع الإثم إذا كان الإذن بالعمل للغير.

In the same way, whether the medical-aid company provides treatment or not — for in some months no treatment will have been required — it is entitled to its payment like a private employee, and it may also accept work from others.

A second answer: Leaving hire aside, a second answer may also be given. Concerning the contract of clientage (walāʾ al-muwālāt) the jurists state that if the lower client commits an offence, the upper patron has to pay the blood money, and if the lower client dies, the upper patron receives the inheritance — even though the lower client may never commit any offence at all, the patron still receives the inheritance, because he has entered into a contract of surety.

Consider what al-Durr al-Mukhtār states:

(أسلم رجل) مكلف (علي يد آخر ووالاه أو) والي (غيره) الشرط كونه عجمياً لا مسلماً على ما مر وسيجيء (على أن يرثه) إذا مات (ويعقل عنه) إذا جني (صح) هذا العقد (وعقله عليه وإرثه له)

(الدر المختار: ٦/۱۲٦، فصل في ولاء الموالاة، سعيد).

Ḥāshiyat al-Ṭaḥṭāwī ʿalā al-Durr al-Mukhtār states:

قال إبراهيم النخعي رحمه الله: إذا أسلم الرجل على يدي رجل ووالاه فإنه يرثه ويعقل عنه وهذا قول علمائنا الثلاثة

(حاشية الطحطاوي على الدر المختار: ٤/۷۰، كوئته).

This also removes the objection that the person paying his contributions into the medical-aid scheme did not fall ill during that month.

The gist of the answer is that the company takes its monthly payment on the basis of a contract of surety, and even if the person paying does not fall ill, taking that money is valid — just as was mentioned in the contract of clientage, where the patron receives the inheritance despite no offence being committed, and the jurists have held that taking that inheritance is valid.

Among the Urdu fatwa collections, Mawlānā Muftī Rashīd Aḥmad Ludhyānwī (may Allah have mercy on him) gave a brief ruling of impermissibility on medical aid in Aḥsan al-Fatāwā. Its text is as follows:

Question: In America the facilities for medical treatment are entrusted to private institutions, etc.

الجواب باسم ملہم الصواب: It is not permissible. واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی اعلم (Aḥsan al-Fatāwā: 7/25).

It is possible that the Muftī Ṣāḥib (may Allah have mercy on him) wrote that it is impermissible because of the indefiniteness of the work; but in other places the Muftī Ṣāḥib held such indefiniteness of the work to be sound where it does not give rise to dispute. Regarding the giving of bread as the wage for baking bread he mentioned two objections: (1) the wage comes out of the very work performed; (2) the wage is undefined. To the second objection he answered that when indefiniteness of the wage does not lead to dispute, it does not invalidate a contract of hire. واللہ اعلم (Aḥsan al-Fatāwā: 7/313).

Indefiniteness that does not lead to dispute does not invalidate a contract; most of the jurists have written this.

Consider what Fatāwā ʿĀlamgīriyyah states:

وأما شرائط الصحة ومنها أن يكون المعقود عليه وهو المنفعة معلوماً علماً يمنع المنازعة فإن كان مجهولاً جهالة مفضية إلى المنازعة يمنع صحة العقد وإلا فلا. (الفتاوى الهندية: ٤/٤۱۱).

ʿAllāmah Anwar Shāh Kashmīrī (may Allah have mercy on him) made a valuable observation:

إن الناس يعاملون في أشياء تكون جائزة فيما بينهم على طريق المروءة والإغماض، فإذا رفعت إلى القضاء يحكم عليها بعدم الجواز، فالاستقراض المذكور "أي استقراض البعير" عند عدم المنازعة جائز عندي، وذلك لأن العقود على نحوين: نحو: يكون معصية في نفسه وذا لا يجوز مطلقاً، ونحو آخر: لا يكون معصية وإنما يحكم عليه بعدم الجواز لا فضائه إلى المنازعة فإذا لم تقع فيه منازعة جاز. (فيض الباري: ۳/۲۸۹، كتاب الوكالة).

The gist of this passage too is that when a contract does not involve disobedience, indefiniteness alone cannot be held to be a cause of invalidity unless it produces dispute.

ʿAlī Aḥmad al-Nadwī says in Jamharat al-Qawāʿid al-Fiqhiyyah:

الجهالة ليست بمانعة لذاتها، بل لكونها مفضية إلى النزاع، وهذا أصل مهم ينبغي التعويل عليه في الأحكام، فإن به حل كثير من المشكلات، وليعلم أن أحكام المعاملات الشرعية مبنية على أصلين عادلين:

الأول: منع كل ما فيه ظلم وأكل لا موال الناس بالباطل.

الثاني: منع ما يؤدي إلى الاختلاف والنزاع بسبب الجهالة، فإذا انتفي ما يؤدي إلى الظلم والنزاع بسبب الجهالة، صح التعامل، والعرف أصل عظيم يرجع إليه في ذلك بعد الشرع. (جمهرة القواعد الفقهية في المعاملات المالية: ۱/۳۱۹، تحت القاعدة: الجهالة إنما توجب الفساد إذا كانت مفضية إلى النزاع المشكل).

Muftī Walī Ḥasan Ṣāḥib (may Allah have mercy on him) says:

The general rule is that indefiniteness which is a cause of dispute is prohibited, and indefiniteness which does not become a cause of dispute is not prohibited. (درس الهداية: الجزء الثالث، ص ۲۹).

Nowadays arrangements of a mixed kind resembling medical aid are current among people, and they have gone on adopting them. For example, in order to escape the affliction of thefts and robberies, people make an agreement with a company: "We shall give you such and such a sum every month, and you are responsible for our security." Then, if a car or the like is stolen, the company traces the vehicle by means of its equipment, or has an announcement made on the radio, and tries to recover the vehicle from the thief. In this case too, the security or service received in exchange for the money paid is undefined, and sometimes for years on end no occasion arises to take that service. Yet because no quarrel arises and it is not a cause of dispute, this too is permissible; and the case of medical aid should be understood in the same way.

There is a further aspect to medical aid. Companies of this kind are mostly not commercial concerns: they are concerned with profit only to the extent of their employees' salaries, and they regard their work as a favour and a voluntary benefaction. It is probably also written in their regulations that they operate on the basis of mutual assistance. On this basis we may also bring this transaction under a conditional gratuity (tabarruʿ mashrūṭ): the company gives assistance and does a favour by way of treatment, and in return a sum of money is paid to it. The jurists have held a conditional gratuity to be permissible.

In Imdād al-Aḥkām there is a detailed question, the substance of which is this: if a company makes an arrangement with a shopkeeper that "if you purchase ten thousand rupees' worth of goods from our company, three hundred rupees will be given to you as a bonus; but if you purchase even once from another company that is a rival of ours, you will not receive this concession" — is it permissible to stipulate such a condition or not?

The answer given is: this transaction is permissible, because the commission given per hundred at the completion of the year is a gratuity on the part of the seller. It is not a binding right of the buyer, and it is permissible to make a gratuity subject to a condition. (Imdād al-Aḥkām: 3/386).

In another place it is recorded — Question: Is it permissible for madrasahs to take an admission fee and a monthly fee from students or not?

The answer: It is permissible, because this is not a wage but a donation, and a condition is permissible in a donation, since no compulsion follows from it: whoever does not accept the condition has the option of not enrolling.

ودليله أنه ﷺ قال لمن أضافه وعائشة رضي الله تعالى عنها قال: لا، قال: فلا إذن حتى قال في الثالثة: وعائشة رضي الله تعالى عنها، قال: نعم

(Imdād al-Aḥkām: 3/606).

The substance of this question and answer is: how can the admission fee be called permissible, seeing that it is not the wage for teaching, which is separate? The answer given is that it is a conditional gratuity — our granting admission is made conditional upon the payment of a donation — and there is no compulsion in it, because the child's father has the option of not enrolling the child at all.

Similarly, Abū Bakr (may Allah be pleased with him) bought a camel saddle from ʿĀzib (may Allah be pleased with him) and asked him to tell his son al-Barāʾ to carry the saddle along with him. ʿĀzib (may Allah be pleased with him) said: No, only on condition that you relate to us the story of the migration. So Abū Bakr (may Allah be pleased with him) related it, and al-Barāʾ (may Allah be pleased with him) carried the saddle.

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī states:

عن البراء رضي الله عنه قال: اشتري أبو بكر رضي الله عنه من عازب رضي الله عنه رحلاً بثلاثة عشر درهماً فقال أبو بكر رضي الله عنه لعازب رضي الله عنه: مر البراء فليحمل إلى رحلي فقال عازب رضي الله عنه: لا، حتى تحدثنا كيف صنعت أنت ورسول الله ﷺ حين خرجتما من مكة والمشركون يطلبونكم قال: ارتحلنا من مكة الخ. (رواه البخاري: ۱/۵۱۵، في مناقب المهاجرين).

This is a conditional gratuity, for which there is room in the sharia. In the same way, in medical aid the undertaking of responsibility for treatment is made conditional upon the payment of contributions. In short, whether we bring it under hire or under a conditional gratuity, both transactions are permissible; neither of them is impermissible.

An objection: A question comes to mind here. Whether this transaction is classed as hire or as a gratuity for a consideration, in both cases the strong presumption about medical companies is that they will be involved in interest-based business and in taking interest from banks. What then is the ruling on treatment into which an element of interest enters?

The answer: The answer is that in non-Muslim countries medical-aid companies generally belong to non-Muslims, and there are three positions concerning the business and transactions of non-Muslims:

(1) Imam Zufar (may Allah have mercy on him) holds that if a hostile unbeliever concludes a transaction contrary to the sharia in the abode of war, it is invalid, because the address of the Qurʾān and the hadith is general. However, no action is taken against the people of war because they are in the abode of war, nor against the protected non-Muslims because of the treaty. Accordingly, wealth that a non-Muslim has obtained by an impermissible means — whether from interest or from gambling and wine — is not permissible for a Muslim to take.

(2) In the view of Imam Abū Yūsuf and Imam Muḥammad (may Allah have mercy on them), if a hostile unbeliever concludes a transaction contrary to the sharia in the abode of war, it is not invalid, because they have not bound themselves to observe the rulings of Islam. As for the protected non-Muslims who live in a Muslim country, their transactions will be held to the laws of Islam; and if their transactions are contrary to the agreed laws of Islam, a judgement of invalidity will be passed upon them, and the money obtained thereby is then not lawful for a Muslim. If they obtained money through interest, that money will not be lawful for a Muslim.

(3) The position of Imam Abū Ḥanīfah (may Allah have mercy on him) is that the unbelievers, whether they live in the abode of war or in the abode of Islam, are not bound by the rulings of Islam without an undertaking or a treaty. In detail: the unbelievers of the abode of war are far removed from observing the rulings of Islam because they live in the abode of war; and as for the protected non-Muslims living in the abode of Islam, they have not bound themselves to those rulings which are contrary to their own religion, custom or law, nor have they undertaken to act upon the rulings of Islam. So the transactions such people conclude contrary to the Islamic sharia will not be called invalid, and it will be permissible for Muslims to take from what they earn. Yes: if the people of the covenant sign an agreement with the Muslims, or accept a condition, which embodies an Islamic law or ruling, then acting contrary to it will be called invalid, and it will then not be permissible for Muslims to take or consume it. Non-Muslims living in non-Muslim countries are like the people of war, so their transactions that accord with their own law will not be called invalid, and the profit obtained from them may lawfully be taken by a Muslim. Ḥaḍrat Mawlānā Ẓafar Aḥmad ʿUthmānī (may Allah have mercy on him) has thrown detailed light on this in Imdād al-Aḥkām, and that fatwa was reviewed by Ḥaḍrat Thānwī (may Allah have mercy on him).

It appears from the text of Imdād al-Aḥkām that Mawlānā Ẓafar Aḥmad ʿUthmānī (may Allah have mercy on him) gave his fatwa on the view of Imam Abū Ḥanīfah (may Allah have mercy on him). Consider the following:

The upshot of the answer is that although the unbelievers are addressed by the subsidiary rulings in matters of penalties and transactions, a general address is not sufficient for a judgement of prohibition and invalidity; an undertaking is a further condition. The people of war have not undertaken the rulings of Islam at all, neither in what accords with their own belief nor in what opposes it. Therefore, in whatever way they earn money — whether from interest, from usurpation, or from void and defective sales, and whether by a way that accords with their religion or one that opposes it — in every case that money and the like enters into their ownership, and it is permissible for a Muslim to take it as salary. (Imdād al-Aḥkām: 4/390).

The text of al-Hidāyah, however, indicates that in the matter of interest Muslims and unbelievers are equal, that is, interest-based dealings are prohibited for both. So if the greater part of a medical-aid company's business, or the whole of it, is interest, one should avoid taking assistance from such a company; but if the greater part of its business is lawful, then there is no harm in benefiting from it and having treatment through it.

Consider what al-Hidāyah states:

والربا مستثنى عن عقودهم لقوله عليه السلام: ألا من أربي فليس بيننا وبينه عهد. (الهداية: ۲/۳۳۸).

(The response to this passage of the author of al-Hidāyah has been given in detail under the heading of the disposal of interest, and may be consulted there).

Fatāwā Qāḍīkhān states:

إن كان غالب مال المهدي من الحلال لا باس بأن يقبل الهدية ويأكل مالم يتبين عنده أنه حرام لأن أموال الناس لا تخلو عن قليل حرام فيعتبر الغالب، وإذا مات عامل من عمال السلطان وأوصي أن يعطي الحنطة للفقراء قالوا: إن كان ما أخذه من أموال الناس مختلطاً بماله لا بأس به وإن كان غير مختلط لا يجوز للفقراء أن يأخذوه إذا علموا أنه مال الغير وإن لم يعلم الآخذ أنه من ماله أو مال غيره فهو حلال حتى يتبين أنه حرام. (إمداد الفتاوى بحواله قاضيخان: ۳/٤۹٤).

These texts show that if the lawful predominates over the unlawful there is no harm in dealing with such a company and in obtaining treatment through it; but if the unlawful predominates over the lawful and it is a Muslim company, then dealing with it is not correct.

واللہ سبحانہ و تعالی اعلم
