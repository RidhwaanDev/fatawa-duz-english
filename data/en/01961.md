### TITLE
Buying and selling shares

### QUESTION
What do the learned scholars say about the following matter? Nowadays shares are bought and sold a great deal. Is it permissible to trade in shares or not? That is, if someone buys some shares intending to sell them at a profit should their price rise, what is the ruling on that? بینوا توجروا ۔

### ANSWER
In the present age the buying and selling of shares has become an important business and a profitable one financially. Shares are sometimes bought directly from the company, sometimes taken through agencies, and sometimes people privately sell the shares they have bought to someone else. All three of these forms are lawful and valid. In every one of them the owners of the shares have either taken possession of the shares themselves, or the manager and administrators of the company have, as their agents, taken possession on their behalf of the underlying ownership represented by the shares.

For anything to be bought and sold it is necessary that the seller either be its owner himself or be the owner's agent authorised to sell; and both of these are found here.

Further, this buying and selling is not a buying and selling of the money for which the purchasers of shares have received a document. It is rather a title to assets, and a buying and selling of those assets, whose initial price the company had fixed. So now, even if they are sold at some difference, and however much more or less than the original price they are sold for, no interest arises.

Once the buying and selling of shares is established as permissible in principle, the ruling then depends on the nature of the company. If the company carries on a lawful business, buying its shares will be permissible, whether the real owners of the company are Muslims or non-Muslims. If it carries on an unlawful business contrary to the sharia — such as making liquor, making statues, and the like — then buying its shares will not be permissible.

Some necessary points to note in trading in shares:

(1) Possession must already have been taken of the share certificate, or the share-holder's ownership in the company to the extent of his shares must in some way have been established with certainty. The sign of this is that if the company were, let us suppose, to suffer a loss, liability for that loss would in law fall upon the share-holder as well. Accordingly it is not permissible to sell shares on without actual possession of them, or without ownership having been established with certainty.

Note: In our times share trading mostly takes place over the internet. As soon as the shares are bought, the money is deducted from the buyer's account and notice of ownership of the shares reaches him by email; after that the buyer can also print out the receipt for the purchase of the shares, which is equivalent to constructive possession. The seller's certificates, moreover, are considered cancelled from that very moment. So there should now be room for him to sell them on, even though the certificates have not yet been received.

The Sharia Standards have declared transfer into an account over the internet to be constructive possession.

إن كيفية قبض الأشياء تختلف بحسب حالها واختلاف الأعراف فيما يكون قبضاً لها، فكما يكون القبض حسياً في حالة الأخذ باليد أو النقل أو التحويل إلى حوزة القابض أو وكيله يتحقق أيضاً اعتباراً وحكماً بالتخلية مع التمكين من التصرف، ولو لم يوجد القبض حسياً، فقبض العقار يكون بالتخلية وتمكين اليد من التصرف، فإن لم يتمكن المشتري من المبيع فلا تعتبر التخلية قبضاً، أما المنقول فقبضه بحسب طبيعته. (المعايير الشرعية، ص ۱۲۲).

(2) With some people, buying and selling is in reality not intended at all in their dealing in shares (there is no existence whatever of goods sold or of a price). They have no intention of taking delivery of the certificates, nor do such people take delivery of them; the whole procedure is merely verbal, its purpose, in the end and in its outcome, being to settle the difference. This form too is absolutely unlawful, since it is gambling and speculation.

(3) Spot transactions are permissible, whether with the intention of investment or with the intention of selling the shares and earning a profit.

(4) A short sale — that is, the sale of what one does not own — is not permissible. If the seller does not own the shares and is making a short sale or a blank sale, it will be unlawful and void, being a "بیع مالایملک" (a sale of what one does not own).

(5) If the seller owns the shares and has also taken delivery of them, but completes the sale today by offer and acceptance for a future date — what is called a forward sale, "البیع المضاف الی المستقبل" — then, because it is for a future date, this is not permissible.

(6) A futures sale is likewise impermissible. It is nothing but speculation; mention of it has passed under (2): that is, such a buying and selling of shares in which the taking and giving of the shares is not intended, the aim being merely to earn a profit by settling gain and loss. This too is impermissible.

(7) An important point deserving attention here is that if a share-holder wishes to end his partnership and leave the company, he cannot leave without selling to someone else. But this is agreed with the company beforehand, and it is necessary to keep to an agreement; for this reason there is room to act upon it.

(8) If the company is involved in some interest-based dealings, one should raise one's voice against it at the company's annual meeting.

(9) When the profits are distributed, whatever portion of the profit was obtained from interest-bearing deposits should be given away in charity.

(10) In the view of some scholars it is also necessary, for the shares to be sold at more or less than their nominal price, that the company's assets should not be in the form of cash alone.

(11) The company's rules include the provision that the company will take interest-bearing loans from various banks. If this is so, it is impermissible, and because of it all the partners will be implicated in the sin of taking interest.

But since this interest-based dealing is slight and of small quantity, and is not itself intended but comes about incidentally and by way of subordination — and since large companies often have to take such loans of necessity, on account of government laws or in order to ward off harm (for instance, a reduction in oppressive taxes) — there can therefore be room for it; and if it is a company of non-Muslims, there will be further relaxation in the matter. Ḥaḍrat Mawlānā Ẓafar Aḥmad Thānwī (may Allah have mercy on him) has discussed this at length in Imdād al-Aḥkām (٤/۳۹۷ ۔ ٤٤۰), the upshot of which is that there is room for it, taking account of what predominates. However, whatever slight amount of interest there is in the company's income, the share-owners should give away that much of the profits they receive in charity.

(12) The "allowance" that a company's manager takes along with his salary is of unknown amount. But if it is known as a percentage, there is room for it.
