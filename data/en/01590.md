### TITLE
The ruling on annulling a marriage by apostatising

### QUESTION
A woman has become utterly exasperated with her husband, and the husband will not give her a divorce. Someone suggested a stratagem to her: "Apostatise — we seek refuge with Allah — and your marriage will be dissolved; then become a Muslim again." So she did exactly that. Has this woman's marriage been dissolved or not? And after becoming a Muslim again, may she marry someone other than her former husband or not?

### ANSWER
Our senior scholars (may Allah have mercy on them) have written on this question in great detail in their fatwa collections. In Imdād al-Muftīn — that is, the older Fatāwā Dār al-ʿUlūm Deoband — Ḥaḍrat Muftī Muḥammad Shafīʿ (may Allah have mercy on him) set out the details of this question and the statements of the noble jurists on pages 570 to 575, quoting passages from Fatḥ al-Qadīr, 3/297, "the marriage of the people of polytheism"; Al-Baḥr al-Rāʾiq, 2/230; Al-Fatāwā al-Hindiyyah, "the tenth chapter, on the marriage of unbelievers", 2/317; and Shāmī, 2/403. He then wrote:

If a woman apostatises while the man is a Muslim, then although the marriage will be dissolved, this woman may not marry another man. Rather, she is bound in the sharia, after renewing her Islam, to renew her marriage with that same husband; and in this new marriage the dower will also be set very low. In this new marriage no account is taken of the woman's consent or refusal — even without her consent the new marriage will be valid by the qāḍī's order. This is the fatwa of the scholars of Bukhārā, and it is also the ẓāhir al-riwāyah position recorded in the standard texts and commentaries, and so on.

In answer to another question he wrote that there are three positions in the Hanafi school relating to this issue:

The first is that the marriage is dissolved, but the qāḍī will compel her to renew the marriage and will make her over to that same husband by force. This is the ẓāhir al-riwāyah position mentioned in the standard texts.

The second position is that the marriage is not dissolved at all, as is the fatwa of a great many of the scholars of Balkh and Bukhārā. Al-Durr al-Mukhtār declared it permissible to give the fatwa accordingly; Shāmī likewise transmitted from Al-Nahr al-Fāʾiq that the fatwa is given on this basis, and in Fatāwā al-Qunyah too the fatwa has been given on it.

The third position is the report of the Nawādir: that instead of remaining a wife she is to be kept with that same husband as a bondwoman — this is stated explicitly in Al-Durr al-Mukhtār and elsewhere. All three positions are quoted in detail in Fatāwā Qāḍīkhān, Fatḥ al-Qadīr, Al-Qunyah, Al-Durr al-Mukhtār and Shāmī, and all three agree at least on this: that after apostatising the woman can in no way pass out of her former husband's charge. On the first position she will, after renewing her Islam, be compelled to renew the marriage, and on the third position she will be kept as a bondwoman. But in India, under present conditions, the Muslims have no power to carry out either of these two courses; therefore the fatwa should be given on that second position — namely, that no separation takes place — which is the position acted upon by the scholars of Bukhārā. Hence in the case asked about the woman's marriage has not been dissolved; as a precaution, however, he should not have relations with her without renewing the marriage, though keeping her in his charge is in any case permissible. And Allah Most High knows best. (امداد المفتین: ۲/۵۷٤) ۔

In non-Muslim countries, however, the jamʿiyyahs and bodies of scholars there should give this matter serious thought. If the woman is genuinely afflicted with hardships, and that affliction could serve as a ground for annulling the marriage, then they should as far as possible arrange promptly for the marriage to be annulled in the light of the pure sharia, keeping before them the details set out in Al-Ḥīlah al-Nājizah and similar works, so that the door to apostasy may be closed.
