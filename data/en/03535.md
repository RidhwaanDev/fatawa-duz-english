### TITLE
Taking an insulin injection for diabetes while fasting

### QUESTION
Is it permissible for a diabetic patient to take an insulin injection for diabetes while fasting in the noble month of Ramaḍān, or not — that is, will the fast be broken by it or not? بینوا توجروا ۔

### ANSWER
It is permissible and correct for a diabetic patient to take insulin while fasting, and no defect will enter the fast on account of it, because insulin contains glucose, which goes into the veins and is absorbed into the blood; whereas what breaks the fast is a thing which reaches the cavity of the brain or the cavity of the stomach through the natural openings.

Let us first gather some information about insulin.

Insulin is a hormone which is produced by the pancreas, and the pancreas is a gland which lies in the region behind the stomach. By means of insulin the body obtains glucose, from which it gets energy.

(Glucose is a kind of sugar which is found in a great many carbohydrates — carbohydrates being a class of foods. After eating, the digestive tract breaks the carbohydrates down and converts them into glucose; the glucose is then absorbed into the blood by way of the small intestine.)

Once the glucose has been absorbed, the cells of the body take up the sugar by means of insulin and energy is produced from it. Another function of insulin is that it keeps the glucose in the blood in balance: if the glucose in the blood rises, insulin gives the signal that the excess glucose should be stored in the liver.

The insulin injection helps in both kinds of diabetes. People who suffer from the first kind of diabetes do not produce insulin in their bodies, so they obtain insulin by way of an injection; and people who suffer from the second kind of diabetes also sometimes need an injection in order to keep the glucose in the blood in balance. Every kind of insulin injection does the same work; the only difference is in how quickly it works and how long it lasts.

Insulin is not taken by mouth; rather it is given as an injection with a syringe, or it is put in by means of an insulin pen or an insulin pump, and it is absorbed straight into the blood. It is generally injected into the thigh, the buttock, the arm or the abdomen. (Taken from MyDr.com.au / Healthline.com).

Consider the English passage:

Insulin is a hormone made in the pancreas; a gland located behind the stomach. It allows the body to use glucose for energy. (Glucose is a type of sugar found in many carbohydrates.)

After eating, the digestive tract breaks down carbohydrates and changes it to glucose. Glucose is then absorbed into the bloodstream through the lining of the small intestine.

Once glucose is in the bloodstream, insulin causes the cells throughout the body to absorb the sugar and use it for energy. Insulin also helps balance the blood glucose levels. If there is too much glucose in the bloodstream, the insulin signals it to store the excess in the liver.

Insulin injections help treat both Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes. People with type 1 cannot make insulin so they must inject it and people with type 2 may need it to control their blood glucose levels.

All types of insulin produce the same effect; however, the different types affect how fast and for how long they work.

Insulin is not taken by mouth. It is injected with a syringe, insulin pen or insulin pump and is secreted directly into the bloodstream. It is generally injected in the thighs, buttocks, upper arms, or abdomen (also known as belly).
