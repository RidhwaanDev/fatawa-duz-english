### TITLE
A second objection to the bank's murābaḥah contract, and the ruling on legal devices

### QUESTION
The real purpose and upshot of a murābaḥah sale through a bank is that the bank gave Zayd one hundred thousand rand and will recover one hundred and fifty thousand, which is plain interest. How far is it correct to take the support of murābaḥah in order to make this permissible?

### ANSWER
The buyer Zayd's purpose is not to take a loan. He needs a machine but does not have the money, and there are two ways of obtaining the machine. One is that he takes a loan, pays back an additional amount, and buys the machine with the loan money; this is plain interest. The second way is that he buys the machine through the bank by way of murābaḥah. This second way is not interest but a device for avoiding interest, and the Prophet ﷺ himself taught this kind of device in transactions. The incident is known to everyone in which a Companion bought one ṣāʿ of good dates in exchange for two ṣāʿs of inferior dates, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ declared it to be interest and rejected it, and taught him this device: that he should sell the two ṣāʿs of dates for dirhams, and then, with the dirhams that became due from the buyer, buy one ṣāʿ of good dates. The upshot of the two contracts is one and the same, but by this device the transaction became permissible.

The kinds of legal device and their ruling:

Not every device is impermissible. Rather, a device whose aim is to nullify a ruling of the sharia or to take away someone's right is impermissible in the sharia. If someone, in order to nullify the ruling of zakat, gifts his wealth to his wife a little before the year is complete, and then the wife gifts the whole amount to the husband before the year is complete, the aim of this device is to nullify a ruling of the sharia and to take a knife to the right of the poor; so it is impermissible.

Or take the case of the Children of Israel, who were forbidden to catch fish on the Sabbath. The aim of that ruling was to forbid making the Sabbath a means of obtaining a catch. But on the Sabbath they gathered the fish into various pools and closed off the way back, and then took them on the Sunday. This was a device to make catching permissible, but it amounted to nullifying a ruling of the sharia, because the Sabbath had become the means of the catch. (Indeed, in the view of some scholars they had also been forbidden to build such barriers.)

Or it is like someone being forbidden to raise his hand and strike another, and his starting to kick him instead: in this case the aim has obviously been defeated.

Or take the case of fat, which was made unlawful for the Jews, as is mentioned in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. They began melting it down and selling it; and since melting does not change the reality of a thing — it is still fat — this device was forbidden and impermissible.

There is, however, no harm in a device whose aim is to save someone from something unlawful, as al-Mabsūṭ of al-Sarakhsī states:

"فالحاصل أن ما يتخلص به الرجل من الحرام أو يتوصل به إلى الحلال من الحيل فهو حسن. (المبسوط للإمام السرخسي رحمه الله: ۳۰/۲۰۹).

In the same way the device of the ṣāʿ was taught in the hadith, as is mentioned in the report of the two Shaykhs, al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

Nor is a device forbidden in which the aim is to save an innocent person. According to the commentators, for example, a broom was used instead of lashes to strike the wife of Ayyūb (peace be upon him), so that the oath might be fulfilled. Nor is it forbidden where there is a need to save someone's life, as in the story of the man who was extremely weak and had committed fornication: since he could not bear the lashes, he was struck with a bundle of thin twigs so that his life might be saved.
