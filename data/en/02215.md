### TITLE
Working as a broker on the stock exchange

### QUESTION
Is it permissible to work as a broker on the stock exchange or not?

### ANSWER
The stock exchange rests on the transactions that a broker carries out. Acting as an intermediary in the business of buying and selling shares, a broker connects the sellers of shares with the buyers of shares in five ways:

(1) Spot dealing. This is the ordinary, straightforward form of brokerage in buying and selling: on the strength of his contacts and his information, the broker takes the price of the shares from a person — or fixes some future date for receiving it — buys the shares for him from a shareholder and hands them over to him, and takes a fixed payment (commission) from that person for the brokerage.

(2) Sometimes the buyer does not have the money, so the broker, in order to earn his commission, pays all or part of the price of the shares on the buyer's behalf, buys the shares for him and hands them over. The buyer then has a few days' grace to pay the price without interest, after which the broker charges him interest. In technical usage this is called a sale on margin.

(3) Tempted by the commission, the broker also sells the buyer shares that he does not own, merely on the expectation or the confidence that he will buy them later. This is called a short sale.

(4) The transaction of purchase and sale between the broker and the buyer is referred to a future date. This is called a forward sale.

(5) The broker engages in speculation. In technical usage this is called a futures sale, which has already been explained earlier.

Of all these forms, only the first is permissible. The remaining four are impermissible, because they involve a defective sale, gambling (speculation), or interest.

So if a person working on the stock exchange as a broker can keep himself from committing those transactions that are impermissible and unlawful, then it is permissible for him to be employed in such an institution. If that is not possible, he should not do the work of broking shares, and should keep himself from falling into what is unlawful.

Jadīd Fiqhī Mabāḥith states: Those who act as intermediaries for buying and selling in the stock-exchange market are called brokers. In the present day such a man has a knowledge of the buying and selling of shares and of their prices, and records the transactions of purchase and sale. Such a person has the standing of an agent and of a hired man who serves several clients (ajīr mushtarak); in other words, a broker is what is called a dallāl. If such people work as agents and brokers for companies that carry on lawful business, this is permissible in the sharia, and their earnings will be lawful in the sharia. (Jadīd Fiqhī Mabāḥith: 16/126).
