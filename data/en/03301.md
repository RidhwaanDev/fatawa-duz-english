### TITLE
The ruling on the ritual bath when urine is drawn off by catheter

### QUESTION
A woman had her urine drawn off by means of a catheter. Does this make a ritual bath necessary or not? بینوا توجروا

### ANSWER
A catheter is a method of treatment in which a tube is inserted into the patient's private part and urine is drawn out through it. This procedure does not make a ritual bath necessary. The ablution, however, is broken, because urine has come out. Consider what is recorded in al-Durr al-Mukhtār with Radd al-Muḥtār:

ولا يجب الغسل عند إدخال أصبع ونحوه كذكر غير آدمي وذكر خنثي وميت وصبي لا يشتهي وما يصنع من نحو خشب في الدبر أو القبل علي المختار، وفي الشامي: قوله على المختار قال في التجنيس: رجل أدخل أصبعه في دبره وهو صائم اختلف في وجوب الغسل والقضاء، والمختار أنه لا يجب الغسل والقضاء؛ لأن الأصبع ليس آلة للجماع فصار بمنزلة الخشبة، ذكره في الصوم. (الدر المختار مع رد المحتار: ۱/۱٦٦، سعيد).

Fatāwā Dār al-ʿUlūm Deoband records:

(1) A man deliberately inserted his finger into a woman's urinary passage. In such a case, has a ritual bath become necessary for the woman or not?

(2) If one woman inserts her hand or finger into another woman's body in order to administer medicine or to examine an internal complaint, or does so for no particular reason, will a ritual bath become necessary or not?

Answer to (1) and (2): No ritual bath is necessary in either case. (Fatāwā Dār al-ʿUlūm Deoband [ʿAzīz al-Fatāwā]: 1/181, Karachi)

Aḥsan al-Fatāwā records:

Zayd inserted his finger, whether with a cloth on it or without, into his wife's private part. Is his wife's ablution broken or not? Answer: The ablution is broken, whether there was a cloth on the finger or not, because when the finger is withdrawn there is bound to be impurity on it, and the emergence of impurity breaks the ablution. If, however, the finger did not enter the inner passage — that is, the round opening — the ablution is not broken. (Aḥsan al-Fatāwā: 2/20, Saʿīd)

Bihishtī Zewar states:

If a woman, overcome by desire, inserts into her own private part the private part of a man who is without desire, or of an animal, or a piece of wood or the like, or the organ of a hermaphrodite or of a dead person, or her own finger, a ritual bath becomes obligatory upon her, whether or not any fluid is discharged. This, however, is the view of the commentator on the Munyah; according to the established position of the school, no ritual bath is necessary without a discharge. (Bihishtī Zewar, p. 869, part eleven, Dār al-Ishāʿat)

Consider the words of the commentator on the Munyah:

وفي وجوب الغسل بإدخال الأصبع في القبل أو الدبر خلاف والأولى أن يوجب في القبل إذا قصد الاستمتاع لغلبة الشهوة

الخ. (شرح منية المصلي، ص ٤٦، ط: سهيل).

Here, however, the object is not pleasure but medical treatment, and so there is no strong ground for holding that a ritual bath is necessary.

Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil states:

If a person undergoes an operation and a special tube is fitted through which urine passes out, then the passing of urine through that tube also breaks the ablution, because impurity breaks the ablution whether it comes out from the appointed place or from anywhere else. (Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil: 1/92, Nuʿaymiyyah)

It records elsewhere: Introducing semen into a woman's womb through a tube does not make a ritual bath necessary. Inserting such a syringe or tube is like inserting a finger into a woman's womb; and just as that does not make a ritual bath necessary, neither will the insertion of a tube. (Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil: 1/99)
