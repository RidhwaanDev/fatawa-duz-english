### TITLE
The link between the literal and the technical meanings of taqlīd

### QUESTION
What is the link between the literal and the technical meaning of taqlīd? If it is said that, just as placing a collar around the neck of a sacrificial animal is called taqlīd, so following the imams means making the imams' fiqh a rope around one's own neck — how far is such a statement correct? Is there not an insult to taqlīd in this comparison, in that a person who follows an imam is being likened to animals?

### ANSWER
Taqlīd means to place a collar around someone's neck. When this collar is around a person's neck it is called a necklace, and when it is around an animal's neck it is called, in Urdu, a paṭṭā. In any case, the collar is not something peculiar to animals. ʿAllāmah al-Ifrīqī writes in the famous dictionary Lisān al-ʿArab:

والقلادة ما جعل في العنق يكون للإنسان والفرس والكلب والبدنة التي تهدي. (لسان العرب: ۳/۳٦٦).

It is hard to say why our ghayr muqallid brothers (those who reject following a school of law) are so attached to the animal's collar, when in the Noble Qur'an the word qalāʾid is used for those consecrated animals around whose necks a collar of honour has been placed — which Ibn Manẓūr al-Ifrīqī expressed with the words:

"والبدنة التي تهدي"

Since the follower places the garland of his devotion around the neck of his imam, he is called a muqallid.

The technical definition of taqlīd has been reported in various wordings. The best definition is the one in Kashshāf Iṣṭilāḥāt al-Funūn. Consider it:

التقليد اتباع الإنسان غيره فيما يقول أو يفعل معتقداً للحقيقة من غير نظرٍ إلى الدليل كان هذا المتبع جعل قول الغير أو فعله قلادة في عنقه من غير مطالبة دليل. (كشاف اصطلاحات الفنون: ۲/ ۱۱۷۸).

In Sharḥ Nāmī on al-Ḥusāmī it is defined as follows:

التقليد اتباع الغير على ظن أنه محق بلا نظر في الدليل. (شرح نامي، ص: ۱۹۰).

Ḥāshiyah Nūr al-Anwār states:

التقليد اتباع الرجل غيره فيما سمعه يقول أو في فعله على زعم انه محق بلا نظر في الدليل. (حاشية نور الأنوار، رقم الحاشية: ۱۸، ص ۲۲۰).

The well-known ghayr muqallid scholar Mawlānā Thanāʾullāh Amritsarī, after giving the definition of taqlīd, writes in Fatāwā Thanāʾiyyah:

Mawlānā Ashraf ʿAlī Thānawī expressed the sense of all these definitions in this way: taqlīd is to accept a person's statement simply out of the good opinion that he will tell one what accords with the evidence, and not to demand the evidence from him.

(فتاوى ثنائية: ۱/ ۲٦۰).

Since the follower places the garland of devotion around the neck of his imam, this act is called taqlīd; and because taqlīd and the collar are used of human beings as well, there is no insult in it. Furthermore, even if there were a comparison with an animal, the thing compared does not possess every attribute of what it is compared to. In "زيد كالأسد", for instance, only courage is intended, not the whole appearance. And if some ghayr muqallid feels an insult in the word taqlīd, what answer will he give to the hadith in which the word taqallud, or taqlīd, is used of a human being?

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī states:

"فتلقاهم النبي ﷺ على فرس لأبي طلحة عري وهو متقلد سيفه فقال: "لم تراعوا، لم تراعوا" (صحيح البخاري: ۱/ ٤۲٦، ٤۲۷).

Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī states:

"وإذا بلال متقلد سيفه"

In this hadith the word is used of Bilāl (may Allah be pleased with him), and in the previous hadith of the Prophet ﷺ. Can any sensible, clear-headed person draw out of this some aspect of disrespect? It is plain, then, that this word is not confined to animals. Nor do the lexicographers regard it as confined to animals. Consider Tāj al-ʿArūs Sharḥ al-Qāmūs, which states:

(وقلدتها قلادة) بالكسر وقلاداً بحذف الهاء (جعلتها في عنقها) فتقلدت (ومنه) التقليد في الدين. (تاج العروس: ٤/٤۷۵).

It seems that when the ghayr muqallidīn could find nothing else, they fired off this objection for no good reason, and in doing so provided proof of their crooked understanding and their lack of knowledge. May Allah grant them guidance and rid them of the bad habit of hunting for faults in what is good. Āmīn.
