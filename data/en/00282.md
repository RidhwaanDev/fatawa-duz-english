### TITLE
Wiping over Reef socks

### QUESTION
Is it permissible to wipe over the Reef company's "Flipper socks" (Flipper) or not?

### ANSWER
The Reef company's diving socks, which are also called seal skin socks, meet every condition for thick cloth socks in the fullest way. They cover the ankles; they stay in place on their own without being fastened; the threads, rubber and so on that hold them in place are part of the sock itself and not something external; and one can walk a farsakh (three miles) in them without shoes. Some muftis in fact tested this by walking in them, and the socks came through completely sound and undamaged. Water does not seep through them either. Some people tested this as well by putting a sock on the hand and dipping the hand into water: not the least wetness was felt on the hand. Indeed, when water was poured into one it held the water like a glass. When washing them, washing the outer surface is not remotely enough for the inner surface; they have to be turned inside out and washed. They also have a strong plastic lining inside which cannot be seen from the outside.

On this basis, wiping over these socks is in our view permissible and valid.

Mufti Niẓām al-Dīn (may Allah have mercy on him), the mufti of Dār al-ʿUlūm Deoband, writes:

There is room to permit wiping over non-leather socks as well — whether nylon, cotton or anything else — provided they have the particular qualities and characteristics of leather socks. Those characteristics are: that leather socks cover the feet along with the ankles, so that no part of the covered limb is visible from above; that the moisture of the wiping does not reach inside; and that if one walks in them without shoes and without tying them, one can walk a mile or two in such a way that they neither tear nor slip down from the shin. So any non-leather sock — nylon, cotton or wool — that has these qualities, that is, one thick enough that the moisture of the wiping does not reach the body and strong enough that one can walk a mile or two in it without shoes and without tying it, and it neither tears nor comes off the foot, may be wiped over just like leather socks. (Niẓām al-Fatāwā: 1/42).

Ḥaḍrat Mufti Farīd Ṣāḥib (may Allah have mercy on him) says:

If nylon socks are available in which all these conditions are met, then according to the view adopted for issuing rulings it will be permissible to wipe over them. (Fatāwā Farīdiyyah: 2/104).

ʿAllāmah Shams al-Ḥaqq Afghānī (may Allah have mercy on him) says:

On the question of thick socks, consider the following passage of the books of fiqh, from Radd al-Muḥtār, p. 188, under the discussion of thick socks:

بحيث يمشي فرسخاً ويثبت على الساق بنفسه ولا يري ما تحته ولا ينشف (الدر) وفي الدر وفي بعض الكتب ينشف وفسر في الخانية الأول بأن لا يشف الجورب الماء إلى نفسه كالأديم والصرم وفسر الثاني بأن لا يجاوز الماء إلى القدم وقال تحت "بنفسه" أي من غير شد

This passage shows that for wiping over thick socks to be permissible, real thickness must be present, and this is established by three things: (1) a man can walk three shariah miles or more in them without shoes; (2) they cling to the shin without being tied; (3) if water is poured on them, it cannot get inside. (Fatāwā Farīdiyyah: 2/104).

Thin socks, however, may not be wiped over, so this ruling should not be applied to cotton or woollen socks of every kind.

Our friend Mufti Shabbīr Ṣāḥib, mufti and teacher of hadith at Darul Uloom Bury, England, made an excellent point.

He said that if there is no room to wipe even over socks as strong and thick as these, the question arises whether the socks over which the jurists and hadith scholars recorded that wiping is allowed have ever existed anywhere in the world at any time, or whether they were describing a purely imaginary case.

This humble servant says that modern products and modern cloth are far stronger than those of earlier times. So when permission to wipe over strong socks existed in earlier times, permission should apply all the more in our own day. We know that fifty or fifty-five years ago strong khaddar cloth would tear within a year or a little more, whereas the cloth of today shows no sign of tearing for years on end. One man used a suit of clothes for about thirteen years and then gave it away to someone.
