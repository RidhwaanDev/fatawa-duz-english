### TITLE
Third-party insurance

### QUESTION
Is third-party insurance permissible or not? Its substance is that some liability may fall upon a person in the future, and insurance is taken out in order to meet that liability. For example, when a car is brought onto the road there is a risk that, as the result of an accident, loss may be caused to someone else, in which case financial compensation becomes binding upon the driver; so insurance is taken out against this, and at the time of an accident the insurance company pays the compensation. This is generally called third-party insurance. In some Western countries it happens that if a person does not clear the snow in front of his house and someone slips on that snow and suffers bodily injury, he sues the householder and recovers heavy damages from him; to escape this risk too, house owners take out insurance. ### ANSWER
There is a difference of opinion among the scholars concerning third-party insurance. Some scholars call it impermissible; for instance, Muftī Taqī (may his blessings continue) says that insurance of every kind is forbidden and impermissible because it comprises interest and gambling — see: (Fatāwā ʿUthmānī: 3/328; Islām awr Jadīd Maʿāshī Masāʾil: 7/290; and Islām awr Jadīd Maʿīshat o Tijārat, p. 161). Some others incline towards permissibility.

Consider what Jadīd Fiqhī Mabāḥith states:

If an accident has occurred through the driver's fault and a person has died in it, then in the light of the sharia the responsibility for paying the blood-money for accidental homicide falls upon the driver. But from where is he to pay so large a sum in blood-money? Islam made the ʿāqilah (his clan) a partner in paying the blood-money, but in the present age the clan system no longer survives, and a driver is generally not able to bear this burden. So the civilised world of today, by putting this method of third-party insurance into practice, has provided a very great facility. This insurance is in reality a form of mutual assistance. It is another matter that certain corrupt elements have also become part of its system; but on that basis a transaction that is entered into to meet an unavoidable social necessity cannot be declared impermissible.

In short, third-party motor insurance is an important necessity of the present age and a transaction of a permissible nature, even though corrupt elements have also become part of its system — the sin of which rests upon those who run that system. (Jadīd Fiqhī Mabāḥith: 4/296–297, compiled by Qāḍī Mujāhid al-Islām Qāsimī (may Allah have mercy on him)).

Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil states:

This humble one is of the view that this form of insurance ought to be permissible in every society in which a system of clan liability (maʿāqil) does not practically exist for such occasions. In this form of insurance there is no question of interest at all, since the person insured receives no money back if no accident befalls him. There is, however, a single element of risk, in that if he remains safe from an accident he will have to forgo the sum he deposited, while if an accident befalls him he will receive assistance greater than that sum. Yet on reflection, risk of this degree exists in the system of clan liability itself, and in walāʾ al-muwālāt, and in certain other transactions as well. The truth of the matter is that this form of insurance belongs to the category of gratuitous donations: the one who pays the insurance instalment offers a donation for the benefit of others of his own calling, and if he himself is ever afflicted, he benefits from the help of others of his calling. (Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil: 4/120).

The reality of this transaction is that there is no aspect of interest in it, for the reason that the insurance company does not pay money over but rather has the other party's car repaired, so this is a consideration for a service. There is, however, gharar (uncertainty) in it, in the sense that it may be that the car never has an accident, so that payment is certain on one side and not certain on the other. The answer to this is that this is slight gharar which does not lead to dispute; and it is clear from the statements of the jurists that in transactions slight gharar is tolerable and does not invalidate the contract.
