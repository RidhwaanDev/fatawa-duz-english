### TITLE
Spending the money from a sacrificial hide on a mosque

### QUESTION
A man sold the hide of his sacrificial animal, and he sold it with the intention of giving the proceeds in charity. May that money be spent on a mosque or not?

### ANSWER
It is not permissible to give the money obtained by selling the hide of a sacrificial animal to a mosque with the intention of charity, because it carries the ruling of zakat, and in zakat ownership must be transferred to a poor person, whereas a mosque is not itself a poor person.

Al-Durr al-Mukhtār states:

فإن بيع اللحم أو الجلد به أي بمستهلك أو بدراهم تصدق بثمنه. (الدر المختار كتاب الأضحية: ٦/۳۲۸، سعيد).

(كتاب الهبة: والصدقة كالهبة لا تصح غير مقبوضة). باب المصرف للزكاة وجازت التطوعات من الصدقات وغلة الأوقاف لهم. (الدر المختار، باب المصرف: ۲/۳۵۱، سعيد).

These passages show that it is necessary to give the price of a sacrificial hide in charity, and that transfer of ownership is also required in it, since it is like zakat. Spending the price of a sacrificial hide on a mosque is therefore not permissible.

Some Afghan scholars say that the price must be given in charity only where the hide is sold with the intention of making money, for then a taint and a defect enter it; but if it is sold with the intention of charity, no defect enters it — although, because of that intention of charity, they do hold that ownership of it must be transferred. These scholars further say that this money is not like zakat or the charity of Eid al-Fitr; it is rather a substitute for the meat, and with meat transfer of ownership is not required: a person may eat it himself and may feed it to others. In earlier times at Mina people would take a little after slaughtering and leave the rest, which is certainly not a transfer of ownership, and this was the practice for centuries: birds and dogs would eat the meat, or it would simply rot. Why, then, should this be turned into something like zakat for no reason? The other jurists require transfer of ownership only so that the door to buying, selling and trading in hides is not opened and so that they do not become a means of amassing wealth. In short, this money is not like a vow or like zakat; it is money that must be given away in charity for an extrinsic reason (واجب التصدق لغیرہ), and so there is no harm in spending it on a mosque. A parallel is this: if someone performs a vowed prayer after the Fajr or ʿAṣr prayer, or prays the two units of ṭawāf at that time, it is disliked, even though necessary prayers may be performed after Fajr and ʿAṣr. The reason is that such a prayer is necessary for an extrinsic reason — that is, its obligation depends on an act of the servant — so it carries the ruling of a voluntary prayer and its obligation is only incidental (کما فی الدر و الطحطاوی: ۱/۱۸۱). Likewise, if a person invalidates a voluntary prayer and then performs it after ʿAṣr or Fajr, that is disliked. This shows that an incidental obligation carries the ruling of what is voluntary in origin. Here too, the meat and hide of the sacrifice do not in origin require transfer of ownership; they are like voluntary charity, and may even be given to a wealthy person. Yes, after the sale an incidental obligation to transfer ownership arises because of the seller's corrupt intention. So when there is no corrupt intention, and he sells the hide with the intention of charity, then, since in origin it is voluntary charity, he may spend its price on a mosque. On the face of it the point made by the Afghan scholars is a fine one, and for their region it also amounts to a juristic preference on grounds of need (استحسان للضرورۃ). But since the position of our elders of Deoband agrees with the plain sense of the books of fiqh, in this humble servant's view, if the person offering the sacrifice gives the whole hide to the mosque before selling it, and the trustee of the mosque then sells it, its price may be spent on the mosque. The mosque can become the owner of the hide, and this is not a waqf either, so the money may be spent on the other needs of the mosque and the madrasah.

Consider what Al-Fatāwā al-Hindiyyah states:

ولو قال وهبت داري للمسجد وأعطيتها له صح ويكون تمليكاً فيشترط التسليم كما لو قال وقفت هذه المئة للمسجد يصح بطريق التمليك إذا سلمه للقيم كذا في الفتاوى العتابية. (الفتاوى الهندية: ۲/٤٦۰).

Shāmī states:

لو غرس شجرة للمسجد فثمرتها للمسجد. (فتاوى الشامي: ۱/٦٦۱، سعيد).

The research of Mawlānā ʿAtīq Aḥmad Qāsimī comes to the same conclusion: that sacrificial hides are altogether different in nature from zakat and the obligatory charities, because the definition of zakat does not apply to them. The reason is that a sacrificial hide is neither part of the nisab nor must it be given to a poor person; the owner may use it himself, and only spending its price on himself is forbidden. Nor is there any restriction in it as between Hāshimīs and non-Hāshimīs. Moreover, even though it is charity, there is no question of cutting off one's own benefit from it: if, for example, a person endows a prayer mat to a mosque, it is perfectly correct for him to pray on that mat himself. (Summarised from Zakāt awr Masʾalah-i Tamlīk by Mawlānā ʿAtīq Aḥmad Qāsimī, teacher at Dār al-ʿUlūm Nadwat al-ʿUlamāʾ, Lucknow; for fuller evidence see pp. 151–155).

Some scholars also distinguish between obligatory charity and property that must be given away in charity. They say that in obligatory charity, such as zakat and the charity of Eid al-Fitr, transfer of ownership is necessary; but in property that must simply be given away, transfer of ownership to a poor person is not necessary — it is enough to take it out of one's own ownership, and that is achieved by giving it to a mosque. This is why Shāmī (may Allah have mercy on him) wrote that a wealthy finder may spend money he has found on the shrouding and burial of a deceased person, because such money must be given away in charity but is not obligatory charity. (Shāmī: 2/338, Saʿīd).

Likewise, Ḥaḍrat Thānwī (may Allah have mercy on him) wrote, concerning stones fixed on a grave whose owner is unknown, that they carry the ruling of lost property, and the ruling for lost property is that it be spent on some good cause. (Imdād al-Fatāwā: 2/587, cited in Fatāwā ʿUthmānī: 3/132).

Since this money too must be given away in charity, with no taint attached to it, spending it on a mosque should likewise be permissible.
