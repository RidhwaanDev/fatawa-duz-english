### TITLE
The sharia ruling on post-mortem examinations

### QUESTION
What is the standing of a post-mortem examination in the sharia? Does it amount to dishonouring the deceased or not?

### ANSWER
It is not permissible or correct to do anything to a dead body that would cause pain to a living person. A post-mortem involves cutting the body up, and since such treatment would be harmful if carried out on a living person, it is not correct with a dead body either. It amounts to dishonouring the human body, and one should as far as possible keep one's dead from it. If it has to be done out of compulsion, however, there is room for it. Consider what ʿAṣr-i Ḥāḍir ke Fiqhī Masāʾil states:
The post-mortem examination of a dead body is no longer merely a medical necessity; in the field of law and justice too, recourse to it for the investigation of crime has become unavoidable. The question that now arises is how there can be any room for a procedure that cannot be carried out without cutting and tearing open the human body. (ʿAṣr-i Ḥāḍir ke Fiqhī Masāʾil: p. 74; Jadīd Masāʾil kā Sharʿī Ḥal: p. 248)

Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil states:
A post-mortem is also permissible if it becomes unavoidable in view of some genuine need — for example, when the cause of death has to be established for the investigation of a case, or when someone has donated an internal organ, the scholars have given a ruling permitting it, and the organ therefore has to be removed, and so on. A parallel to this is that the jurists have permitted the abdomen of a dead pregnant woman to be cut open when the child in her womb is still alive, so that the child can be taken out. (Jadīd Fiqhī Masāʾil: vol. 1, p. 203, "Post-mortem", Progressive Books)

Kifāyat al-Muftī states:
Many kinds of medical examination (post-mortem) take place without any sharia necessity, and these are impermissible. Even if a particular case is permissible because of a sharia necessity, the rules of sharia concerning covering the body and honouring the deceased must still be observed. There is no doubt that tearing and cutting open the body of a deceased person is contrary to the respect due to it, and until there is a reason strong enough for that violation of honour to be overlooked, such cutting cannot be lawful. As for the uncovered body of a woman, quite apart from passing into the hands of a man who is not a close relative, it may not even come under his gaze. (Kifāyat al-Muftī: 4/200, chapter eight, Kitāb al-Janāʾiz, Dār al-Ishāʿat; Niẓām al-Fatāwā: 1/412, "The ruling on post-mortem", Islamic Fiqh Academy; Imdād al-Fatāwā: 1/508).
