### TITLE
How an Islamic bank works

### QUESTION
How an Islamic bank works.

### ANSWER
A bank's activities are generally divided into two basic parts.

(1) The liabilities side:

On this side an interest-based bank takes money from its depositors, and for this purpose it introduces various kinds of account. Basically there are two kinds of account:

(a) The non-profit-bearing account, which is called the current account.

(b) The profit-bearing account, which includes the savings account, the fixed deposit and so on.

(a) An account of the non-profit-bearing account, that is, the current account:

The money that an Islamic bank takes as a current account is, in the sharia, an interest-free loan, because the account holder is guaranteed that his money will certainly be returned, and every guaranteed sum is called a loan. No additional amount is paid on it either; only the original sum is returned. It is therefore equivalent to an interest-free loan.

(b) An account of the profit-bearing account:

The money that an Islamic bank takes for accounts other than the current account — the savings account, the fixed deposit and the like — is taken on the basis of muḍārabah or mushārakah (partnership). In this the depositor has the position of rabb al-māl, "the person who provides the capital", or of a sleeping partner, while the Islamic bank has the position of the muḍārib, or working partner.

Whatever profit the Islamic bank earns through its modes of finance — murābaḥah, ijārah (hire), mushārakah and so on — it gives a proportionate share of it to its depositors, and this is settled in advance. For example, it is agreed that of whatever profit the bank earns, fifty per cent will go to the bank and fifty per cent to the depositor. In the sharia the depositor's money is held by the bank as a trust: that is, if the money is lost without any wrongdoing, shortcoming or negligence on the bank's part, the bank is not liable for it.

It should also be remembered that when an Islamic bank takes capital from a depositor it cannot guarantee its return in every event, nor can it state definitely at the outset how much profit will be received. Indeed, it is not even permissible to fix a ratio of the capital and tell the depositor a definite figure; that makes the partnership defective. In any case, an Islamic bank generally does not itself know how much profit it will earn on that capital.

If, however, at the end of one of its terms the bank gives the depositor a proportionate share of the profit that has actually been earned, then working out the ratio of that realised profit to the original sum and stating it is not impermissible in the sharia.

From this we see that if an Islamic bank, after distributing the profit, announces that this year it gave its depositors a ten per cent profit, this practice does not clash with the principles of the sharia. But if at the outset it says, "We shall pay such-and-such a percentage of the capital as profit," that is impermissible in the sharia.

(2) The second part is the assets side:

On this side the bank provides various financing facilities to those it finances.

That is, the bank provides financing facilities to people who approach it for their various financial needs:

In an Islamic bank various transactions are carried out in view of the different needs of the client (the person taking a loan from the bank).

Nowadays three kinds of transaction are generally the most common:

(1) murābaḥah; (2) ijārah; (3) diminishing mushārakah. Besides these, financing facilities are sometimes provided through salam (advance purchase) and istiṣnāʿ (a contract to manufacture).

(1) Murābaḥah:

Murābaḥah is in fact a kind of sale in which the owner of the goods tells the buyer what the goods cost him and how much profit he is adding in selling them to him.

The murābaḥah carried out in Islamic banks consists of the following stages:

(a) The master agreement: at the first stage the client and the bank make a comprehensive agreement between themselves, which is called the general agreement. In it they settle how much worth of goods the client will buy from the bank, how much profit the bank will take on the goods purchased, what the method of payment will be, and so on.

(b) Purchase of the required goods: after this the bank buys from the market the goods that are later to be sold to the client. Generally the client is made the bank's agent for buying the required goods, but this need not always be so.

(c) Taking possession of the goods purchased and informing the bank: if the client is made the agent for the purchase, then after buying the required goods he takes possession of them and informs the bank that, as its agent, he has bought these goods and taken possession of them. Since in the sharia the agent's possession is the principal's possession, the goods will be regarded as being in the possession of the principal, that is, the bank. At this stage, therefore, all the rulings of possession apply — in particular the ruling that if the goods are destroyed without any wrongdoing on the client's part, the loss falls on the bank and not on the client.

(d) Conclusion of the murābaḥah: after this the client offers to buy the goods from the bank at a fixed price which includes the cost and the bank's profit, undertaking to pay that price either at once or after a specified period. When the bank accepts the offer, the murābaḥah comes into being and payment of the price becomes due from the client. Against this payable price the bank takes certain securities from the client.

(2) Ijārah:

In the terminology of the sharia, ijārah is the name for giving a specified and lawful use of a thing or of a person in return for a specified payment.

The difference between ijārah in an interest-based bank and in an Islamic bank:

Hire transactions take place in both interest-based and Islamic banks, so the differences between the two are set out here.

In the hire arrangement current in conventional banks at present there are the following three faults:

(a) Within a single contract there are two transactions, a sale and a hire. That is, the instalments which the client pays during the period of hire are counted at first as instalments of rent, but as soon as the period of hire is complete these instalments are treated as the price, and the item automatically becomes the property of the client. In the terminology of fiqh this is called ṣafqatān fī ṣafqah (two transactions within one transaction), which is not permissible in the sharia.

(b) All the responsibilities connected with the item hired out fall on the lessee, whereas in the sharia only the responsibilities connected with its use may be placed on the lessee — such as having the car serviced and the oil changed — while the responsibilities that belong to ownership fall on the lessor, such as paying the tax, and having the item repaired if it is destroyed by some accident.

(c) Rent begins to run on the item hired out even before it is handed over to the client, whereas in the sharia it is not permissible to take rent from the lessee until the item in question has been handed over to him.

In the hire arrangements of Islamic banks, by contrast, the defects mentioned above are removed in the following way:

(a) At the outset there is only a hire transaction, and the item hired out remains the property of the bank. Then, when the period of hire ends, the client has the choice of buying it at a fixed price or returning it to the bank. In the first case the bank sells the item to the client by a separate contract, and sometimes the bank gifts the item to the client by a separate contract. In this way the fault of two transactions within one transaction does not arise.

(b) In the hire transactions of Islamic banks it is expressly stated that the lessee will bear only those responsibilities that relate to the use of the car — this is termed ṣiyānah ʿādiyyah (ordinary upkeep) — while the bank bears all the responsibilities that belong to ownership of the car. So the tax on it, its insurance and takāful, and making good any damage to the car in the event of an accident are the bank's responsibility.

(c) An Islamic bank does not collect rent until it has made the hire contract and handed the item over to the client.

(3) Diminishing mushārakah:

The third major mode of finance current in Islamic banks today is mushārakah mutanāqiṣah, "diminishing partnership", through which finance is generally provided for houses; for this reason it is also commonly called "home mushārakah". This arrangement basically consists of three stages:

(1) At the first stage the Islamic bank and its client jointly buy a house, and the bank's share in it is generally larger than the client's. For example, a house is bought jointly in such a way that eighty per cent of it belongs to the bank and twenty per cent to the client.

(2) The bank's share is divided into small units. In the example given, the bank's share is made into eighty units. The client goes on buying the bank's units one by one, with the result that the client's ownership keeps increasing while the bank's ownership decreases.

(3) For as many units as remain the bank's property, the client goes on paying rent under a hire agreement, since he keeps them in his own use and enjoyment. Because the client is steadily buying units, the amount of rent also keeps falling. Finally, when the client has bought all the units owned by the bank, he becomes the owner of the whole house.

So in this arrangement three things basically take place:

(1) The joint purchase of the house.

(2) One partner's taking on hire the share owned by the other partner.

(3) One partner — that is, the client — buying the share of the other partner, that is, the bank.

For this contract to be sound it is necessary that no one transaction be made conditional upon another. In the arrangement described, no transaction is made conditional upon another; rather, the client of his own accord gives a one-sided undertaking that if the bank buys the house in question, he will take the bank's share on hire and pay rent for it, and that he will buy the various units of the bank's share stage by stage.
