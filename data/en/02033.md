### TITLE
The kinds of bank card and their ruling in the sharia

### QUESTION
(1) How many kinds of bank card are there, and what is the ruling on them? Does it make any difference to the permissibility or impermissibility of a credit card whether there is money in the account or not?

(2) What is the ruling on drawing cash with a credit card?

(3) What is the ruling if the bank charges the card holder a duty or a commission?

(4) When a purchase is made with this card, interest is applied if the amount of the bills is not paid within the fixed period. Does the condition of interest render the contract defective or not?

(5) The bank sometimes gives credit card holders a prize. Is it permissible to accept it or not?

### ANSWER
Three kinds of card are in use at the present time:

٭ (1) Debit Card:

The holder of this card already has an account with the institution whose card he has obtained. Whenever the card holder uses the card, the institution makes the payment out of the money in his account. He is given no credit facility with it; he can use the card only so long as there is money in his account. The institution charges a fee for issuing the card.

Using this card is beyond doubt permissible, and buying and selling with it is correct, since it involves neither a loan nor interest. It is, however, the card holder's responsibility not to use the card in matters contrary to the sharia.

٭ (2) Charge Card:

The holder of this card does not already have an account with the institution; rather the institution provides him with a credit facility. He is given credit for a set number of days, within which he must pay the institution. If payment is made within that period no interest is charged; but if the card holder does not pay on time, he then has to pay with interest. The institution charges a fee for issuing the card.

It is permissible to use this card on the following conditions:

(1) The card holder must make full arrangements to pay before the fixed time, so that no possibility remains at any point of interest being imposed.

(2) It is the card holder's responsibility not to use the card in matters contrary to the sharia.

(3) If his need is met by a debit card, it is better for him not to use this card.

٭ (3) Credit Card:

The holder of this card likewise has no account with the institution; the agreement itself is one of credit at interest. In this agreement the institution does grant a fixed period within which the card holder need pay no interest if he settles his account. But the agreement is in essence based on interest, and there is a promise to pay it. Besides this, the card carries a rescheduling facility, which extends the period of payment; but along with this the rate of interest rises, and in some cases an extra sum is charged.

There are several kinds of credit card: (1) American Express, (2) Visa, (3) MasterCard, (4) Euro Card, (5) Diners Club, and so on.

The ruling is that if a debit card or a charge card is not available, using it is permissible subject to the conditions given above for a debit or charge card; otherwise it is not permissible. But since the likelihood of interest in it is strong, it should be avoided.

Answer (2): There are two ways of drawing cash with a credit card.

The first is to draw it by hand: the card holder presents his card to the bank staff, and on seeing the card they hand him the cash. This is permissible on condition that no extra charge is taken for issuing the cash, because such a charge would be against the loan, and that is interest.

The second way of drawing cash with a card:

Drawing it through an A T M (Automated Transfer Machine). These machines are themselves very costly, and large sums are further spent on their installation, security and the like.

The ruling is that if the institution charges a fixed sum for the use of the machine as a fee for using it, without regard to the amount drawn, this is permissible; but if the institution charges something calculated on the amount drawn, this is not permissible and will be interest. The institution may, however, charge a fee for issuing the card.

Answer (3): It is permissible for the bank to charge the card holder a duty and a fee, because this is a fee for services — for example, issuing the card, preparing it, putting the markings on it, processing the files, the cost of telephone calls and the like, and carrying out every kind of office procedure and service; and then installing cash machines in every large city, which are themselves costly machines. It is plain that a fee is being charged for these services, so there is no harm in it.

Answer (4): The application of interest. It should be clear that the bank's contract with the credit card holder is a contract of loan: the money given by the bank is a debt owed by the card holder, and the defective condition of interest on the loan is attached to it (namely, that if the money is not paid within the fixed period, interest will have to be paid). But a loan is one of the gratuitous contracts, not a contract of exchange, and the principle among the Hanafis is that attaching a defective condition to a contract of exchange renders the contract defective, whereas in a gratuitous contract the defective condition is itself void and the contract is not rendered defective — though the wrong of attaching a defective condition does follow. If, however, a person makes a firm resolve that this defective condition will never be acted upon, and that he will pay the amount of the bills within the fixed period and will not let it come to paying interest, then, if God wills, there will be no sin in this defective condition.

Answer (5): The ruling on a prize given by the bank.

It is permissible to accept and to use the prizes that banks and companies give to card holders, because the bank stands in the position of the lender, and when a lender gives his debtor a prize it is permissible for the debtor to accept it. If, on the other hand, the debtor gives the creditor a gift or prize (on account of the loan), it is not correct for the creditor to accept it.

(Drawn from Krēḍiṭ Kārḍ kē Sharʿī Aḥkām by Mawlānā Muḥammad Usāmah; a fatwa by Muftī Muḥammad Fārūq, Dār al-Iftāʾ, Jāmiʿah Iḥtishāmiyyah, Karachi, pp. 130–132; Fatāwā ʿUthmānī: 3/354; likewise ʿAṣr-e Ḥāḍir kē Pēchīdah Masāʾil awr Un kā Ḥal, compiled by Mawlānā Mūsā Karmāḍī: 2/195–199; and Jadīd Muʿāmalāt kē Sharʿī Aḥkām: 1/142–146).

It is permissible for the bank to give a prize and for the holder to accept it. This kind of contract is called a gratuitous contract, that is, doing someone a favour subject to a condition. Mawlānā Ẓafar Aḥmad ʿUthmānī wrote of its permissibility in Imdād al-Aḥkām, 3/386.

There are also several supporting precedents:

(1) A Persian neighbour invited the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) to a meal, and he accepted on the condition that ʿĀʾishah (may Allah be pleased with her) be included. See: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim: 2/176.

(2) Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq (may Allah be pleased with him) bought a saddle from ʿĀzib (may Allah be pleased with him). Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq said to ʿĀzib, "Tell your son to carry this saddle to my house." ʿĀzib (may Allah be pleased with him) said, "No, not until you relate to us the story of the migration." So Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq (may Allah be pleased with him) related the account of the migration.

عن البراء رضي الله عنه قال: اشتري أبو بكر رضي الله عنه من عازب رضي الله عنه رحلاً بثلاثة عشر درهماً، فقال أبو بكر رضي الله عنه لعازب رضي الله عنه: مر البراء فليحمل إلى رحلي، فقال عازب رضي الله عنه: لا، حتى تحدثنا كيف صنعت أنت ورسول الله ﷺ حين خرجتما من مكة. (رواه البخاري: ۱/۵۱۵، مناقب المهاجرين).
